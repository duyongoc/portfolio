# Project — duyongoc.github.io

A portfolio for a Unity/Unreal game developer, built as a room you look around
rather than a page you scroll. This file documents how it is put together, why
the load-bearing decisions were made that way, and where the remaining edges
are. `README.md` is the short introduction; everything operational -- how to
run it, the checks, the bake steps -- is here.

- **Live:** https://duyongoc.github.io/
- **Host:** GitHub Pages, static, no build step, `.nojekyll` present
- **Entry:** `index.html` redirects to `room-3d.html`

Both HTML pages are single files: markup, CSS and JS inline. That was deliberate
for a static host with no bundler — one request, one parse, nothing to wire up.

**The argument is about bundling, not about files, and it is worth being clear
which.** The page has run `<script type="module">` for as long as it has used
three.js, and a static host serves an import graph with no build step at all —
so "no bundler" never actually required one file. `demos/room/` exists for the
pieces that can be lifted without reaching back into the room, and `sound.js` is
the first: 275 lines that read exactly two values from the rest of it. The bar
for the next one is the same — a piece that gives back more than it imports.
Until something clears it, it stays inline.

### Two front doors, one dataset

`portfolio-data.js` is the single source of truth. Both experiences read it and
`portfolio-shared.js`; neither owns project data of its own. The 2D page is a
real alternative, not a stub: same 33 builds, same links, same categories.

**Its `links` and `tags` are generated.** They are the fields that actually get
edited — a build gets a store page, a WebGL build moves, a video is re-cut — and
doing that in place meant opening 33 entries of JSON to change one URL and then
getting `tags` to agree with it by hand. So `build_links.txt` is the
hand-written record and `tools/links.py` writes those two fields; the rest of it
is still edited there. Same arrangement as `build_layout.txt` -> `wallsheet.py`,
and for the same reason: Pages runs no Python, so the output is committed and
`--check` in CI is what stops a forgotten bake from being a silent no-op. The
first run of `--export` reproduced the committed file byte for byte apart from
two URLs that had a stray leading space, which is the sort of thing a
hand-edited data file accumulates. See `doc_build_links.md`.

**`cat` is the only taxonomy.** A project used to carry a `section` as well —
"RESKIN", "AR Games" — which was a per-project copy of the heading its category
already implies, kept in step by hand across all 33 entries. Measured, it was a
perfect 1:1 map of `cat`: no information, and nothing to stop it drifting. The
heading, the label, the long name and the accent colour are all properties of
the category and all live in `CAT` in `portfolio-shared.js`, which is also
where `tools/wallsheet.py` reads them from rather than restating the colours as
RGB tuples in Python.

**The markup helpers are shared too.** `esc`, `thumb`, `img`, `externalNote`
and `linkRow` were a copy in each page. That is worse than untidy: both pages
load `portfolio-shared.js` as a *classic* script, so two top-level `const esc=`
is a redeclaration error, not a duplicate. `tools/jscheck.py` checks for
exactly that. The copies had already drifted — the flat page's cards were
missing the screen-reader "(opens externally)" note the room puts on every
outbound link.

Routes into the 2D page exist at every failure point — the boot screen's
"Open lightweight archive", the WebGL-unavailable card, and the persistent
`▦ View 2D` button.

### No CDN

three.js and its two loaders are vendored in `demos/lib/`. Nothing is fetched
from a third-party origin at runtime. A portfolio that fails because unpkg is
having a bad afternoon is a portfolio that fails during the one week someone
is looking at it.

### The ceiling

The ceiling is in frame for roughly the top third of the default view, so it is
not a surface that can be left to itself. It carries two flush panels recessed
into it and a cyan LED trim running the perimeter — a deliberately *flush*
vocabulary, nothing hanging.

For a long time three service pipes hung under it as well, 2.4 to 3.3 clear,
and they were the only prop in the room with no argument written down for them.
The argument against them, once someone looked, was the ceiling itself:
exposed plumbing is basement language and this is a glass-walled floor of a
tower, so three grey tubes slung under a finished ceiling — crossing in front of
the one lit panel in it — read as scaffolding nobody took down. They also sat at
z=-25, -19.5 and -14 with no relation to the panels at -18 and -4, which is why
they read as one striped smear rather than three objects.

**Ribs replaced them: the ceiling's own vocabulary continued rather than
contradicted.** Flush to the soffit, dropping 1.1, spanning the full width and
buried 0.7 into both walls so no end hangs in the room. Being structure they
need no hangers, which the pipes needed and never had. Pitch 14 at z=-24.5,
-10.5 and 3.5 interleaves them with the two panels on an even 7 — rib, panel,
rib, panel, rib. The lit slot is a pair of thin quads hugging each rib, inset to
the same 53 the perimeter trim uses, pushed into `ledStrips` so they fade with
the hour for free and cost no light.

Two things worth keeping:

- **`castShadow` had to be turned off, which `box()` otherwise sets.** A rib is
  flush against the ceiling and 55 long, so under any grazing sun it lays a
  shadow band the full width of the room across the very surface it is mounted
  to; three of them between them blacked the ceiling out. The pipes got away
  with it by being thin and hanging clear. Nothing below can see a ceiling rib's
  shadow anyway — it only ever subtracted.
- **On this ceiling, visibility comes from light, not from form.** At the
  grazing angle the camera always sees it from, a flush rib's side faces never
  catch anything; what reads is the lit slot beside it, exactly as the panels
  read because they are emissive. Anything added up here has to glow or it is
  not there.

### Interactive surfaces

Every hoverable thing is a `SCREENS` entry created through `addScreen()`. An
entry carries its own geometry (`w`, `h`, `sc` scene node), its colour, its
label, and a `kind` that selects how its content renders:

| kind | Renders as | Used by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `grid` | tile grid of covers, optionally led by `caseItems` | wall, monitors |
| `list` | two-column rows | showreel |
| `case` | case-study cards with description + links | — (folded into the wall) |
| `jobs` | timeline | Career |
| `contact` | avatar, stats, contact rows | badge |
| `about` | bio + fact grid | — |

`bodyFor(s)` is the single switch that turns a screen into HTML. Adding a
surface means adding a `SCREENS` entry, not touching the render loop.

**`SCREENS` is not the same list as `PANELS`.** Every entry is raycastable and
glows under the pointer; only `PANELS = SCREENS.filter(s => !s.noPanel)` opens
anything. The index, the prev/next ring, the `3/10` counter and the `#panel=`
deep links all walk `PANELS`. Three surfaces are `noPanel` — the centre
monitor, the cat and the penguin — and `activateHit` swallows their clicks while
`setHot` withholds the pointer cursor, so the glow says "this responds to you"
without the cursor promising a panel that is not there. All three say everything
they have to say in the room itself; none of them carries cover tiles, which is
why `activateHit` needs no second branch here any more. The two side columns
used to be in this list and are not — see the side-wall section.

**The centre monitor carries the employer, not the work.** It used to hold the
case files, on the reasoning that the camera points at it. It now shows the
Athena Studio lockup, and the case files moved to the wall — where they are
already hanging as cover art, which is a better place to read about them than a
screen on the other side of the room. The wall panel therefore leads with
`caseItems` prose and follows with the full enumeration, under two `.sh` section
rules.

That whole arrangement rests on one claim: every card in the lead is also a
cover on the wall behind it. `FEATURED` is written by hand and the layout moves
covers, so the two drifted — two of the six stopped hanging on zone 1 and the
panel went on leading with them, which put a set of six at the top of a page
about eleven with a third of it about builds that are not on that wall. It is
an intersection now, in the wall's own order, so the claim holds by
construction; the heading says which (`4 of the 11 on this wall`) rather than a
bare count. A featured build that is not on the wall keeps its tile in the
archive below and loses the lead — `wallsheet.py` warns that its `FEATURED`
entry no longer does anything, which is the half of this a tool can still tell
you. This also puts the richest content behind the hero's
`ALL 33 BUILDS` button, which previously opened a bare grid.

**The archive under it reads in the wall's order.** `items` was `games` —
portfolio-data.js order, which is a maintenance order grouped by category, so
the wall's first cover was the panel's third tile and its fourth cover was the
panel's thirty-third, with Shooter2D above Demo and Turnbase above Adventure.
Zoom into a wall and the page that opens has to be that wall's page: the
visitor has just read eleven covers in a particular order and the tiles under
them are the same eleven, so finding the one they pressed should be a glance
and not a search. `gWall` is `zWall.items` followed by everything the wall does
not hang — the same 33, permuted. The two side columns never had this to fix;
they pass `Z.items` through as their `items` because they hold nothing else.
`zWall` is the single `zoneOf('wall')` the plane, `gCase` and `gWall` are all
cut from.

### The panel: two placement modes

This is the most consequential piece of the interaction, and it now behaves
differently by device class.

**Desktop — projected placement.** `placePanel()` projects the four corners of
the target surface through the camera, takes the screen-space AABB, positions
`#panelSlot` at those pixels, and applies CSS `zoom` so the panel's logical
width fills that rectangle. `zoom` rather than `transform: scale()` on purpose:
a transformed subtree is rasterised and then enlarged, which makes every label
soft. `zoom` re-lays-out and re-renders text at its final device-pixel size.

**The logical width is derived, not a constant, and that is the whole point.**
It used to be 680px landscape / 440px portrait. Because `focusOn` parks the
camera at `max(dH,dW) * 1.2`, every landscape surface settles at the same
projected width — `innerWidth / 1.2` — so at 1440x900 *every* panel came out at
**zoom 1.76** and the phone prop, which is height-limited, came out at **0.88**.
Two consequences, and the second is the bad one:

- The panel was a 1.76x magnifying glass. Body copy authored at 11px rendered
  at 19px; the Contact panel spent the screen on a 374px avatar and pushed the
  actual contact rows below its own fold.
- **The container query never saw more than 680px.** `@container panel` reads
  the logical width, so a panel occupying 1200 real pixels laid itself out as
  if for a narrow window — two case files, four grid columns — and then blew
  that result up. The layout was picked for a size the panel never was.

`panelWidth()` now calls `restingProjW(s)`, which builds the pose `focusOn` is
flying toward (`restPose()`, shared by both so they cannot drift), projects the
quad through a scratch camera, and returns the width the panel will actually
be. Logical width is that over `PANEL_ZOOM` (1.06), clamped to 300…1560.
Measured after: **1.06 on every panel** at 1280x720, 1440x900 and 1920x1080.
One step of magnification is the room's voice — the panel is a screen you
walked up to. Two steps is a magnifying glass.

Computed once at open, not in `placePanel`: that runs on every frame of the
fly-in, and a logical width moving with it would re-run the container queries —
and relayout the whole panel — on each one.

Because the query now sees real widths, two rules were added above the existing
`max-width` ones: `.cs` goes to three columns at 900px and four at 1300px.
`PANEL_MIN` is 300 rather than a desktop figure on purpose — a panel on a
small prop should be a narrow page at native size, not a desktop layout
squeezed into 300 pixels. The phone prop that made this concrete is gone (see
the desk pass), so the AR tablet is now the narrowest surface and the QA
harness's narrow anchor.

**Mobile — bottom sheet.** On a phone held upright the monitor projects to a
band across the middle of the screen, so the projected panel occupied roughly a
fifth of the viewport while the bottom half showed a keyboard. Below
`MOBILE_GPU`, `placePanel()` short-circuits to `sheetRect()`: full width, 82%
of viewport height, pinned to the bottom, `zoom` of 1. The camera fly-in still
happens — the metaphor pays for the trip, not the read.

The layout inside the panel responds to `@container panel` queries on `#glass`,
not to viewport media queries, so it adapts to the panel's own width in either
mode.

**The flight is timed in seconds, not in frames.** The camera's four eases —
the open ramp `fB`, the orbit's `yaw`/`pit`/`dist`, and the `fPos`/`fLook`/`gUp`
chase — were written as a fixed fraction of the way there *per frame*, which
makes the frame rate the clock. That is 1.48 s of flight at 60 Hz, 0.74 s on a
120 Hz panel and 2.96 s on a machine that has dropped to 30. Worse, the rate is
not fixed for the length of one flight: the frames during a zoom are the most
expensive the room renders — a panel laying out, thumbnails decoding — so the
camera changed speed with whatever the renderer managed that instant, which
reads as an uneven zoom rather than a slow one. `ease(rate, dt)` =
`1 - (1-rate)^(dt/16.67)` converts each to a fraction per 1/60 s; at 16.67 ms it
returns `rate` unchanged, so every constant keeps the value it was tuned to and
60 Hz is bit-identical. `dt` is already clamped to 50 ms, which caps a stalled
frame's step. Every other integrator in `tick` already took `dt`; these were the
last four that did not.

**The bin was the worst of them, for a second reason.** Its readout is the only
card carrying a list, and five of its six rows play a clip. `hudDrop` left them
running for the whole `HUD_SHUT` (545 ms) so the single-clip card could scan off
without freezing — but those 545 ms start on the click that closes the card,
which is the click that starts the fly-in. The bin spent the opening half-second
of its zoom decoding five videos nobody was looking at. `hudDrop` now calls
`hudListStop()` immediately; the rows scan off on their posters. The single
`hudVid` keeps its run-under-the-rewind, which is what that effect was for and
costs one decoder rather than five.

### The wall readout

Hovering a cover on the wall opens `#hud`: a sci-fi panel in the right gutter
carrying the cover art, the category, the pitch and the two links worth
following, with a bracket on the tile it describes and a leader line between
them. `wallCellAt()` resolves the pointer to one of the eleven covers, so the
readout always knows exactly which game it is talking about.

**There are three walls of work now, not one.** The back wall carries the
selected eleven; the left column three and the right one four, eighteen covers
in all. They exist because the
measurement said they could: sweeping a probe quad across the whole orbit, the
left wall around z=-4 renders at 32-34 degrees off square — what the main grid
itself manages — and the right wall reaches 80% visible at the left end of the
drag. One category per column, deliberately: a column of mixed section stripes
reads as leftovers, a single band reads as a shelf. Left is prototypes beside
a poster that already glows magenta, right is my own builds in the purple its
monitor already uses.

The two columns are not the same length, and nothing says they have to be: a
zone owns its own grid, the sheet's proportions follow from it, and the plane
in `room-3d.html` is shaped from that. Three on the left is 1x3 and 0.544 wide;
four on the right is 1x4 and 0.408. Change one and the other is unaffected —
what cannot drift is the plane, which is why the QA sheet compares the baked
image's proportions against the mesh it is mapped onto.

Growing the right column went up rather than in. The width is what decides how
many screen pixels a cover gets, and that wall is solid and full height from
z=-2 forward, so there is room above and below and none to either side.

`Topdown Sword`, `Game3d Racing` and `Game2d Black Ops` are the three builds
with no YouTube link in `portfolio-data.js`, so no preview can be cut for them
at all and a cover of theirs is a still in a room whose whole readout is motion.
That is why the first two were kept off the columns. Everything else that has a
source now has a clip, including the twelve that `SKIP` had rejected — see the
tooling section for what that changed.

**Each column is its own panel, and for a while neither was.** The argument for
`panelAs` was that a handful of tiles does not deserve a page and every build on
them already has one somewhere, so a cover click ran `focusOn` against another
screen — `monR` for the prototypes, `wall` for my own, since the MY GAMES
monitor those three used to open is gone — spotlit on that build. The
destination was right and the journey was wrong. You press a column captioned
"3 more reskins" on the left wall; the camera flies across the room to a monitor
titled something else, holding fifteen builds, and yours is highlighted
somewhere down the scroll. Nothing warns that this is about to happen and
nothing on arrival says that it did, so what it reads as is the room zooming to
the wrong thing — which is exactly how it was reported.

Both columns are in `PANELS` now, so the index and the prev/next cycle carry
them, and a cover click opens the column it is on. What the redirect was
actually for survives as `more`: the id of the archive holding the rest of that
column's kind, rendered by `moreRow` as a labelled row at the foot of the panel
and taking its name, tag and accent from the target screen rather than from a
string written twice. The jump is the same jump; it is a control now instead of
a side effect.

Their panels are the narrowest in the room, because a panel is its surface's
projected rectangle and these surfaces are 0.54 and 0.41 wide-over-tall: 462 and
347 logical pixels at 1080p, against 1358 for the reskin monitor. One column of
cards at both, which is what `cols:1` and the 380px container query already
agreed on.

**Trim on a side wall cannot be careless.** The back wall's panel overhangs its
frame rails by .8 at each end and nothing notices, because the wall runs well
past the panel in both directions. Neither side wall does: the left one runs
into the poster and the right one runs out over the glass at z=-2, and the
first build of both had trim floating on a window and lit strip crossing the
poster. The overhang is 1.2 now, both columns sit as far back as that allows
(z=-3 and z=2.8), and the poster's own light strips were shortened from 16.6 to
15.2 — .8 of lit strip past the frame at each end, invisible while the wall
beyond it was empty, was the thing that collided first.

**The About-me poster moved back three units** to make room, from z=-13 to
z=-15.5. The left wall is only in frame between roughly z=-8 and z=0 and the
poster was lying across the whole of it. A poster reads the same anywhere along
a blank wall; a column of covers that has fallen off the viewport edge does not.

**That move is what put the poster into the wall vent, and both ends of a prop
need checking, not just the one being made room for.** At z=-13 the poster's
back edge landed at -20.5, exactly the vent's front face — touching, so nothing
showed. Moving it to -15.5 pushed that edge to -23, and the vent housing stands
.75 further off the wall than the poster does, so this was not a z-fight it
could win: 2.5 x 2.75 of the poster's top-back corner and 2.6 of its top lit
rail sat behind a metal grille. The whole review of that move was about the
forward end, where the column of covers was going.
Found by the zone patches, incidentally — zone 5 traces the poster exactly and
draws with `depthTest` off, so it painted the collision onto the grille in
flat colour, which is a use for the overlay nobody designed it for.

**Then the fix repeated the mistake in the other direction.** Cutting the vent
from 9 deep to 6 cleared the poster and left it 0.2 inside the back wall,
because again only the end being made room for got measured. It is now sized to
the gap instead of to itself: the band runs from the back wall's inner face at
z=-29.3 to the back edge of the poster's lit rail at -23.1, and 4.5 deep centred
at -26.2 sits in it with 0.85 clear at both ends — half the volume it started
with. Its top is at y=32, level with the top of the back wall's panel, the only
other thing in frame beside it. The housing is buried 0.3 into the left wall,
which is a joint and meant; the blades bite 0.1 into its face and stand 0.35
proud, where they used to bite 0.15 and float 0.35 clear in front of it.
**Size a prop to the gap it has to live in, not to how big it looks right.**

**A vent with nothing coming out of it is the one prop that cannot just sit
there.** Everything else in the room is allowed to be a shape; a vent's whole
identity is that something moves through it. It now has a draught — twelve
alpha-blended billboards on staggered 17-second lives, six on a phone — leaving
the grille face and drifting 5.6 out into the room, spreading into a fan as they
go and dissolving.

Three things were wrong with the very first version, and each is a rule:

- **Additive blending would have made it the ninth light.** Every additive quad
  in this room is a light source and that is why there are so few of them. A
  glowing plume reads as a gas leak lit from inside, not as air. Alpha blend;
  tint what is behind you.
- **It is a cool grey and deliberately not an accent.** Registering the
  materials in `ACCENTS` was the first try, and that curve takes every accent to
  nearly full chroma at midnight — it came out saturated cyan against a dark
  violet wall and read as a lamp. Air has no colour of its own.
- **A sine envelope put all the opacity in the wrong half.** It is down to .45
  by two-thirds of the way out, and by then each puff has also grown to four
  times its birth area, so the alpha was being spread thinner at exactly the
  point it was being lowered. Measured against the wall the far half was under
  .04 — a smudge at the grille and nothing beyond it.

### The props that took several passes

The vent's draught, the bin and its spilled artwork, the cat on the sofa and
the penguin on the chair each took four or more passes, and what was learned on
each is in **[docs/decisions.md](decisions.md)** — including the three cat
models thrown away before this one, why the tilted neck is a vertex weight
rather than a cut and why there is no tail hinge, why the bin's readout plays
six clips rather than listing six stills and what that cost the card's width,
why steam and air are different silhouettes rather than different
densities, and why a translucent effect has to be measured by differencing two
frames rather than looked at.

The rules that came out of them are in § 8 below. The rest is working out, and
there was a third of this file of it — in the file somebody reads before moving
a game onto a wall.


### Time of day

`STOPS` is a keyframe table indexed by hour, carrying sky top/bottom colour,
sun colour and intensity, ambient intensity, lamp level, city-light level and
light-shaft strength. `applyTime(h)` interpolates between the bracketing pair
and drives everything downstream: sun azimuth and elevation, the shadow the
window casts, the patch of sun sliding across the floor, moon and star opacity,
cloud and haze tint, glass reflectivity.

A full day passes every ten minutes. You arrive at a random hour. `#t=20.6`
pins a specific one; the scrub bar takes over on drag and `▶ Resume` hands
control back to real time.

One subtlety worth preserving, documented in the source: the `HemisphereLight`
takes its `color` from `SKY_TOP` (the dome overhead) with only a third of
`SKY_BOT` mixed in. Handing it `SKY_BOT` alone lit every surface from above
with the horizon colour — at dawn that put a salmon film over the whole room
and flattened its contrast.

### Weather

The room always had rain — one sheet, one density, falling whether or not it
made sense under the sky behind it. What it did not have was a sky that changes
its mind, which is most of the difference between a window and a photograph of
one.

`WEATHER` is four states — clear, cloudy, rain, storm — and every field in them
is a **multiplier or an addend on a value `applyTime` already computed**, never
a value of its own. That is the whole design: weather cannot disagree with the
hour about what colour the sky is, because it does not get to say. Deleting the
block leaves the room exactly as it was.

Most of a storm falls out of one line. `sun.intensity` is scaled by `W.sun`,
and `daylight`, `sunVis`, the patch of sun on the floor, `sun.castShadow` and
the city-light level are all derived from it further down — so an overcast dims
the sun disc, pulls the patch off the floor and brings the towers up without a
line each. Ambient gets a much gentler factor (`W.amb`): overcast light is
flatter, not absent, and taking it down with the sun turns a grey afternoon
into dusk.

**The sky is desaturated, not repainted.** Rather than a second keyframe table
of grey skies per hour, `wash()` pulls each sky colour toward its own
luminance. That holds at every hour for free — a stormy dawn is still a dawn,
it has just lost the salmon, and a stormy midnight goes flat instead of turning
into a daylight overcast pasted over a night sky.

**Transitions walk `WSEQ`, they do not jump it** — one step at a time, 17s
crossfade, 34–80s between changes, so a storm never arrives without an overcast
in front of it. **But the direction persists.** Weather has momentum: a front
moves through and moves out again, it does not flip a coin at every step.

That was a coin flip at first, and it is the reason lightning had effectively
stopped existing. A reflecting random walk on four states takes **nine**
transitions to reach the last from the first — about eleven minutes at a minute
a step — so unless a visit happened to open on `storm`, the storm was
theoretical. Momentum cuts it to three or four. Reversal (15%) is rolled before
the end-reflection, not after, so a flip at either end cannot walk off the array.

Arrival is drawn from `WSTART`, weighted the way `STARTS` weights the arrival
hour and for the same reason: the room is at its best in weather, so a uniform
draw across four states spends two visits in seven on a clear sky nobody came to
see. `#w=storm` pins a state and stops the clock.

##### Weighting the hold, not the walk

A sweep along four states is 1, 2, 3, 4, 3, 2 — the two ends are visited once
per cycle and the two middles twice — so with a flat hold the walk spends
**half** its time with nothing falling. Half is too much once temperature is a
coin flip, because that dry half is the half that is neither rain nor snow, and
the three things a visitor is meant to be able to tell apart then split
50 / 25 / 25.

`WHOLD_MUL` moves the time without touching the ordering: `.60` on clear and
overcast, `1.40` on rain and storm. A storm still cannot arrive without an
overcast in front of it — only the length of the stay changes. Weighted by
visits that is .60, 1.20, 2.80, 1.40 out of six, which after the fixed 17s
crossfade dilutes to about **32% dry, 68% with something coming down**.
Simulated at 250 ms steps over 12 runs of 120 h, against the `COLD` odds below:

| month | dry | rain | snow |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| September | 32% | 35% | 32% |
| January | 32% | 32% | 36% |
| July | 32% | 36% | 32% |

The **cycle length is unchanged** — three short holds and three long ones sum
to the six flat ones they replaced, about seven and a half minutes for a front
to cross and come back. Churning faster would have been a different bug.

Simulated over 5000 visits, share that sees at least one strike:

| | 1 min | 2 min | 3 min |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| coin-flip walk, uniform arrival | 25% | 38% | 44% |
| momentum + weighted arrival | 31% | 55% | 70% |

**The rain density formula has two terms and they mean different things.** The
original `city` term made rain faintest by day, because these are additive
sheets and additive vanishes against a bright sky. That was right while rain
was permanent scenery and wrong the moment a storm can happen at noon —
measured, a midday storm sat at .18 and the glass read as merely grey. The
`W.dark` term comes from the weather rather than from the hour, which puts a
midday storm at .28 against clear weather's .037 and leaves clear weather
untouched. The clamp at .34 is not decoration: these sheets sit on the most
overdrawn surface in the room, and past about a third they stop reading as rain
on a window and start reading as fog inside it.

**The rain used to fall upward, and had since it was written.** `applyTime`
scrolls the sheets with `map.offset.y`, and three.js samples `uv * repeat +
offset` — so *raising* offset.y walks the sampled row up the texture, which puts
the streak it belongs to *lower* on the quad. Falling rain is therefore `+=`,
and the room shipped `-=`. Nobody caught it in either direction because at the
old fixed opacity of .065–.15 the streaks were too faint to read a direction
off; turning a storm up to 2.6x the density and 2.4x the speed is what put it on
screen. **Amplifying an effect is a test of the effect**, and it is worth
running deliberately on anything faint the next time one of these is touched.

The sign was settled by measurement, not by argument — the derivation is easy to
get backwards, and was, twice. An isolated plane carrying one bright band moved
it +40px down a 400px viewport for an offset of +0.1, exactly the predicted
fraction of the height; the real room, with the rain layer isolated by
subtracting an otherwise identical `norain` frame, moved +13px down for +0.06.
Correlation-based measurements of this need their sign convention calibrated
against a synthetic shift in the same script, or they will happily report the
opposite.

#### Two sheets, because one sheet is a plane

One sheet of rain is a flat sheet: every streak the same length, the whole thing
sliding at one rate, and what the eye reads is a scrolling texture. The far
sheet has more streaks, shorter and fainter, tiled 1.6x tighter so each lands
smaller on screen, and scrolls at 0.52x. It is not there to be seen. It is there
to *disagree* with the near sheet, and that disagreement is the only cue saying
the rain occupies a volume rather than a plane.

The two are close in material opacity (.30 against .34 in a night storm) only
because the near one is pinned to its clamp; the difference lives in the
textures, whose per-streak alpha runs .05–.17 against .10–.38. Skipped entirely
on `MOBILE_GPU` — of everything on that surface it is the layer whose absence
costs least, being deliberately almost invisible on its own.

#### Gusts

`W.speed` was a constant per state, which made a storm scroll at a
mathematically perfect rate for every second of its life. That is the single
clearest tell that rain is a looping texture, and it was free to fix.

A gust is **not a speed change**. It is a speed change and a lean that do not
peak together — the sheet leans before it accelerates and is still leaning after
it has slowed — so it is two independent sums of sines at rates with no common
period, and the pattern never visibly repeats. Both scale with `bite`, so clear
weather keeps its original dead-calm vertical fall and only real weather
breathes. `windLean` is also what pushes the beads sideways, so one wind moves
both, and the two panes take it with opposite sign because they face 90° apart.

#### Rain on the glass

The falling sheets are rain in the air, on the far side of the window, and
nothing about them says which side of the glass the camera is on. What makes a
room read as **inside** is the water that never gets there: beads that land on
the pane, sit, swell and eventually break loose and run.

A 2D canvas per pane rather than geometry. A hundred and thirty beads are a
hundred and thirty draw calls as quads and one `drawImage` loop as a texture —
and the trails come free, because the canvas is faded toward transparent on each
repaint instead of cleared, so a bead's path stays behind it and dries on its own
without anyone tracking a polyline. Repainted at ~22fps, not per frame.

One canvas *per pane*, sized to that pane's aspect, rather than one shared
texture tiled across both: the two runs of glass are 28 and 16 units wide, so a
shared sheet would either stretch its beads into ovals on the wide pane or tile
visibly on the narrow one. Beads are not a pattern.

**There is no release timer.** A drop runs when it grows heavy enough to beat
surface tension and not before, so every bead carries its own release radius and
its own growth rate, and the pane genuinely gets wetter while you watch instead
of cycling on a stopwatch. It also gets the population right for free: small
drops never run, because in life they never do — every trail on a window comes
from one of the fat ones. Growth rate is the one number here with a budget; a
hundred-odd beads all crossing threshold on a twenty-second cycle is five new
runnels a second, which is not a window in the rain, it is a car wash. Measured
in a night storm, 0–4 running per pane at once.

**The sprite went through three versions and the lesson is about the blend
mode.** These sheets are additive — the only thing they can do is add light —
and a filled disc of added light is a glowing ball. Version one had a bright
rim all the way round and looked like soap bubbles stuck to the window; a full
ring is a bubble, because light entering a hanging drop leaves through the thick
underside and that is the only edge that lights up. Version two fixed the rim
and was still a field of spheres, because the *body* was still a filled disc. A
real bead is a lens, and a lens is as much darker as it is brighter — additive
cannot do the darker half at all. So the body is nearly nothing and the drop is
carried by its highlight, which is also what the eye actually picks out on a
window from across a room. Beads got smaller and far more numerous at the same
time, and only then did they stop being objects and start being weather.

The daylight constant in the bead opacity is deliberately the smallest of its
three terms. Additive white on a grey midday sky is a snowflake, not a raindrop,
and it is only by day that the constant does any work: after dark the other two
already carry the sum past the clamp, so lowering it costs the night nothing.

#### Wetness is the one weather value with memory

Every other field is instantaneous — `W.rain` is whatever the blend says this
frame. Water is not. Glass and asphalt do not dry at the rate the cloud stops,
so `wet` rises over about four seconds and falls over twenty-one: measured, 1.0
to 0.29 in 27 seconds after a hard cut to clear, a time constant of 21.6s
against the 21s in the code. The beads are still running and the deck is still
shining well after a storm has faded to overcast, and that asymmetry is the
whole reason it is a separate value: it is the difference between weather that
happens **to** the scene and weather the scene **remembers**.

It is driven off `W.rain` rather than the state name so it crosses a fade
smoothly, and offset by the clear-sky baseline of .55 so clear weather dries to
exactly zero and costs nothing — beads are `visible=false` below .02, not merely
transparent. `#w=` snaps it rather than ramping it, because a pinned state is
for screenshots and QA and a four-second ramp makes every capture a race.

#### The wet street that was cut

Wet asphalt is darker and shinier at once: the diffuse surface drops because
water fills the pores, the specular rises because a film of water is a mirror.
Doing only the second half made the deck look like somebody turned the
brightness up. Both halves ship, on `groundFX.base` and `groundFX.glows` — the
actual deck material and the actual lane, pad and beacon materials, at the
actual depth, occluded by the actual buildings. In a night storm the markings
run 61% brighter on a deck 34% darker.

What did **not** ship is the sheen disc that was supposed to be the headline: a
flat additive plate on the ground standing in for a reflection. It was measured
twice, by diffing frames against the same frame with the disc scaled to zero:

- at **418** units its bright ring sat 230–418 units out and could not be
  separated from the frame's own churn. From inside, this window shows deck out
  to about the landing pad at 43 units and the towers take everything past it.
- at **260** it registered — and painted straight over the skyline. The city
  layers are transparent materials, so they write no depth, so a flat disc drawn
  after them is not occluded by them. What should have been wet asphalt came out
  as luminous fog banked across the towers, washing out exactly the silhouettes
  the haze lerp works to keep distant.
- at **80**, where nothing is behind it, it moved the mean luminance of the
  ground crop by **0.12 out of 255**.

A sheet big enough to see is a sheet that fogs the towers. The idea was sound
and the room simply has no room for it: from inside, one rim of a landing pad is
all the ground there is. Wetness that changes the materials that are already
there survives; wetness that needs a surface of its own does not.

#### The city was only reacting to weather in daylight

The skyline had three weather terms in it from the start — the silhouettes
washing toward the sky by `W.dark`, the window grids dimming, the haze band
thickening — and measured at noon they work: the city crop runs luminance
**100.2 → 66.4** across clear→storm, a 34% drop. Measured at 21:00 the same
range moves it **37.3 → 32.7**, and almost all of that came from one term.

The primary cue is aerial perspective:

```js
L.sil.color.copy(cC).lerp(SKY_BOT, clamp(L.haze*(.10+daylight*.52) + W.dark*.30, 0, 1));
```

It fades a tower toward the colour of the sky behind it. At noon the sky is
bright, so the wash has somewhere to go. **After dark `SKY_BOT` is already the
silhouette colour**, so it has nowhere to go: across the entire clear→storm
range the far layer moved from `(.009,.009,.022)` to `(.008,.006,.020)`. The
main weather mechanism in the city was a daylight mechanism, in a room whose
best hours are after dark and whose reason to look out of the window is rain.

Four things came out of measuring it, and none of them is a new effect — three
are corrections to numbers that were already there.

**The weather term was flat across the layers.** `W.dark*.30` applied equally
to the tower 330 units out and the one at 132. Air does not do that: it
attenuates by distance, which is the whole reason `L.haze` exists three lines
above. Weighted by the layer's own haze, a storm at 21:00 now takes the far
layer's lit windows down **38%** and the near layer's **13%**, where both used
to lose a uniform 25%. A uniform loss reads as a dimmer; a graded one reads as
depth, and it is free.

**After dark the wash has to run the other way.** A low deck over a neon city
does not darken it — it traps the city's own light underneath, and the far
towers go milky rather than black. So there is a second lerp toward a lit haze,
gated on `night` so noon never sees it and on `L.haze` so it stays a distance
cue. Same clear→storm range at 21:00, far layer: `(.009,.009,.022)` →
`(.015,.031,.064)`. The near layer stays where it was.

**Air traffic had no weather term at all.** Measured at 21:00 it sat at `.4663`
in clear air and `.4658` in a thunderstorm. Worse than nothing: everything
around it dimmed 25% while it did not, so the aircars were *more* prominent in
a storm than on a clear night. Storm now leaves 9% of them flying. This is the
cheapest thing in the list and probably the most convincing, because it is
behaviour rather than exposure — a sky that empties reads as weather to
somebody who is not looking for it.

**The cloud deck scrolled at a fixed rate.** Same tell as a rain sheet at
constant `W.speed`: a sky that crosses the window at one speed in a dead calm
and in a 2.4× gale is announcing that it is a loop.

After all four, clear→storm moves the city crop by **35.9** mean absolute
difference at noon (was 29.7) and **6.4** at 21:00 (was 6.0, against a
same-state noise floor of 0.77) — but the night number understates it, because
what changed there is distribution rather than level.

#### The rain curtain: weather in the city, not on the window

Everything else the rain does happens within a tenth of a unit of the glass.
Between that glass and a tower 215 units out there was nothing at all, which is
the deeper reason a storm could take a quarter off the skyline's brightness and
still read as a dimmer: the room was wet and the city was merely darker. Nothing
was falling *between* them.

A squall at 215 units does not resolve as streaks, it resolves as a travelling
curtain, so that is what it is — one cylinder segment, a 1024×256 canvas of wide
flat wisps, colour taken from the live `SKY_BOT` and pulled 45% toward a lit
grey, opacity `clamp(W.haze*2.2, 0, .42)`.

Two things make it work rather than fog the aperture:

- **`renderOrder = -26.5`.** The layers own -30..-23 in silhouette/lit pairs, so
  -26.5 lands between layer 1's lit pass and layer 2's silhouette. The curtain
  fogs the two far layers and leaves the two near ones sharp. A veil in front of
  everything is a grey filter over the window; a veil at a depth is weather.
- **Normal blending, not additive.** Additive can only add light, so it would
  make the far towers *brighter* through the rain rather than hidden by it —
  the same mistake the beads were fixed for. A curtain has to be able to take
  the city away.

It drifts on `W.speed`, about 15% of its texture in 8 seconds under a storm and
not at all under a clear sky, which is also when it is `visible=false`. It is
the only thing out there that moves *with* the weather; the traffic strip
scrolls at a fixed rate whatever the sky is doing.

Cost, measured by disposing it at runtime and re-counting: **+1 draw call**, and
zero in clear weather. A storm frame at 21:00 comes to 265 draw calls against
270 for a clear one, so the whole set is net-free.

Lightning lights it, unconditionally — only `storm` carries `flash:1` and storm
always carries haze, so there is no state in which that can brighten a curtain
that is not there. A strike lights the rain between you and the city before it
lights the city.

#### Snow is a second axis, not a fifth state

The obvious way to add snow is a fifth entry in `WEATHER`, and it does not
survive contact with `WSEQ`. That walk is ordered on purpose — clear, cloudy,
rain, storm — so a storm never arrives without an overcast in front of it. Ask
where snow goes on that line and there is no answer: nothing comes *between*
rain and snow, because they are the same cloud deciding differently about
temperature.

So temperature is the second axis, `COLD` is rolled **once per visit**, and the
four states render as snow instead of rain when it is set. Nothing downstream
learned a new state. `WSEQ`, the crossfade, the momentum, `wet`, the audio bed
and the lightning leash all keep reading the same `W`. A cold `rain` is snow, a
cold `storm` is a blizzard, and a cold `cloudy` is still just an overcast —
nothing is falling out of it, so it keeps its name in the clock too.

The roll used to be weighted hard by the real month — `COLD_BY_MONTH` ran .62
in January down to .03 in June–August — and that is meteorology, not design.
What it actually bought was three quarters of the year in which the snow, the
accumulation, the caps on every roofline and the second audio bed **could not
occur**: a September visit reached them once in seventeen. Half the room, built
and then made unreachable by a table.

The table is now a **tilt on a coin flip** rather than the flip itself: `.47` in
midsummer to `.53` in midwinter, mean `.498`. It still runs coldest in January,
which is the whole of what the seasonality was ever saying here, but across a
±.03 band instead of a 20:1 swing. The band is that narrow because the ask was
an even three-way split and a wider tilt spends it — at ±.06 a September visit
is already 37% rain against 30% snow.

It stays a probability rather than a calendar for the original reason: a date
test gives three quarters of a year in which this code cannot run at all, and
no way to see it in July without a deep link.

`COLD` is constant for the visit and not animated. Rain turning to snow while
you watch is a lovely thing and it is also a crossfade between two particle
systems with nothing in common — one of them beads on the glass and the other
does not. This is the version that is honest about what it does.

##### The murk belongs to rain, the accumulation to snow

Rain and snow read the *same* `W`. The consequence took three rounds of feedback
to see: the **city outside was identical in both**, and only the sheet on the
glass differed. Two obscured skylines are two obscured skylines — a storm and a
blizzard were being told apart by the shape of the falling particles alone, at
the exact distance where shape stops resolving.

The split is by **kind of obscuration**, not by amount. Rain's murk is
continuous — vapour, spray off the deck, a curtain of water between here and the
far towers — and it is what makes a wet city dissolve at distance. Snow's is
discrete: the flakes themselves, already drawn, in front of air that is
otherwise unusually clear. So `wBlend` scales `W.haze` by 1.45 and `W.cloud` by
1.30 **on the warm branch only**, and what a blizzard gets instead is what has
settled on the rooflines, which rain structurally cannot produce.

Only the warm branch is scaled, and that is a constraint rather than a
preference: thinning the cold one would lift the aperture by taking the curtain
away, and the curtain at night is *brighter* than the sky it covers. Making rain
murkier can only ever darken the window.

Measured on the aperture crop, mean of 255:

| | 13:00 | 21:00 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| clear | 88.96 | 35.87 |
| snow | 71.33 | 39.96 |
| rain | 67.31 | 38.01 |
| blizzard | 65.72 | 42.15 |
| storm | **60.74** | 39.81 |

By day the order is now unambiguous and in the right direction: every weather
darkens the window, and rain darkens it more than snow at both intensities.
After dark precipitation reads *brighter* than a clear sky — additive sheets and
a lit curtain against a near-black sky — and that has always been true here and
is also true out of a real window. What matters is that the cold column is not
brighter than the warm one because of a glow; it is brighter because there is
snow lying on thirty rooftops.

##### A scrolled tile cannot be snow, and the reason is why the rain works

A rain streak is motion-blurred. The eye cannot lock onto an individual drop,
so the moment the tile wraps is invisible, and that is the only reason two
scrolled quads read as rain at all. A snowflake is a discrete dot falling at
maybe an eighth of the speed, and the eye *tracks* it — so the wrap is not
merely visible, it becomes the thing you are looking at.

The split is by what the eye can follow:

- **The mass** is two scrolled tiles, same machinery as the rain, square rather
  than 256×512 because a dot stretched by a non-square repeat stops being one.
  At that size a flake is a speck and specks are interchangeable.
- **The near flakes** are a per-flake canvas sim — structurally `stepBeads` with
  different physics, and deliberately so, because the beads had already proved
  the budget. 40 a pane, repainting at 33ms rather than the beads' 45: a bead
  creeps, a flake travels, and the sway is a sine that stepping shows up in.

##### Three layers that share a size are one layer

The first build of this shipped and could not be told apart from a single
scrolled sheet, which is the exact failure it exists to avoid. Both ladders
overlapped. In world units the near tile's dots ran .035–.115 against the near
flakes' .085–.254 — a *small* flake was smaller than a *large* tile dot — and
the near tile fell at 2.16 units a second against 1.69–3.01 for the flakes, so
the mass was overtaking most of the flakes that are supposed to be in front of
it. Two overlapping populations are one population, and the eye reads one field
of specks.

**And the ladder has to be checked in screen pixels, not world units.** The
first correction separated the three cleanly in world units and still failed,
because it put *both* mass layers under one screen pixel — where a size
difference cannot exist at all. This window is about 13 screen pixels to a world
unit (28 units of glass across roughly 340px), and the conversions run through
three different scales: the near canvas is 384px over the glass's 27 units, the
near tile 256px over 6, the far tile 256px over 3.6.

| band | screen px | fall, units/s at storm |
| --- | --- | --- |
| far mass | 0.16 – 0.44 | 0.55 |
| near mass | 0.79 – 1.68 | 1.20 |
| near flakes | 2.62 – 5.48 | 2.6 – 4.6 |

Gaps between every pair, in both dimensions. The near flakes are also *dimmer*
than the mass (`sk*.72`, sprite centre .70 rather than .95): they are the
out-of-focus ones a foot from the glass, and a lens spreads a flake over more
area rather than making it brighter — at .95 a six-pixel blob burns a hole in
the skyline.

A note on measuring this: the frame-diff that isolated the two motion sources
is nearly blind to the near flakes now (17.2% of pixels moving with everything
running, 18.2% with the flakes frozen — inside the noise). Forty large, soft,
low-opacity blobs change very few pixels by more than 8/255 while being the most
visible thing in the window. The diff proved the *mass* is doing its job; only
looking at it proves the flakes are.

Sway is the whole reason the near flakes cannot be a sheet. A sheet can only
sway as a whole, which reads as the window moving rather than as air. Sway and
wind are separate terms on the flake: the tumble belongs to the flake, the gust
belongs to the room, and both scale with the flake's depth so the far ones drift
less.

Isolated by stopping one source at a time, on the window crop:

| moving | mean abs diff | pixels > 8 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| everything | 4.84 | 17.2% |
| near flakes stopped | 4.71 | 18.2% |
| mass stopped too | 1.16 | 6.5% (noise floor) |

The mass is the snow; the near flakes are the character. That is also why the
near sim can be small — and why this table cannot be used to tune it.

**The sim has to ride `W.speed` like everything else.** On its own clock the
near flakes fell at exactly one rate through light snow and a blizzard alike —
found by zeroing `W.speed` and seeing the window still move as much as before,
which is the same "it is a loop" tell the rain sheets were fixed for. The factor
is shallow (`.55+W.speed*.35`) because snow does not accelerate the way rain
does; it is carried, not dropped.

##### The lean that turned the snow into a texture on the pane

Snow does lean harder than rain, so the sideways drift was set to **3.8× the
rain's** — rain is a vertical thing the wind leans, snow is a horizontal thing
that happens to be descending. Left at the rain's lean a blizzard fell straight
down and read as ash. That much is right and it went much too far, for a reason
that is only visible in the *right units*.

**Check this ratio in world units, never in texture offset.** The rain tile is
16 world units and the snow tile 6, so "a seventh of the rain's rate" in offset
was a **thirty-fifth** of it on the wall. Measured over 30s of gusts, the near
mass ran 0.78 units a second down and 3.35 across: a tiled texture whose
dominant motion is horizontal, sliding 4.3:1 sideways across the pane. That is
not snow in the air, and the report back said so exactly — *the snow looks
stuck to the glass and the glass is moving*.

The fall goes to `.00021` and the lean to `1.6×`. Same 30s window: **1.98 down,
1.41 across**, so a blizzard descends at a steep angle and only crosses past
horizontal at the top of a gust. Rain, for contrast, runs 11:1.

**The near flakes had the same disease in a different form.** At
`H*(4.0e-5 + d*8.2e-5)` the slowest crossed the 27-unit pane in eighteen
seconds while swaying 18px with a period of four — so what the eye received was
a blob wobbling in place, which is a speck *on* the glass, not a flake outside
it. Roughly doubled to `H*(9.0e-5 + d*1.5e-4)`, with the sway cut to `3+r*8` so
it stays small next to the fall: a crossing of **3.1–4.5s**, unhurried for snow
and unmistakably falling.

The parallax ladder still holds, and it is the constraint on all four numbers —
far mass **1.13**, near mass **2.45**, near flakes **5.99–8.69** units a second.
The mass may never overtake the flakes that are supposed to be in front of it.

Snow also gets roughly twice the opacity ceiling the rain sheets get, and that
is not taste either. These are additive quads: a rain streak is a thin diagonal
covering almost none of the pixels it crosses, a flake is a filled disc. At the
rain constants the snow was present and unreadable.

##### Snow that has stopped falling

Falling snow and falling rain look more alike than they should. Both are a
scrolled sheet on the glass, both drive the same `W`, both drop the same veil
between the room and the towers — and the report back was the honest one: *I
cannot tell which weather I am looking at.* Two hazes are two hazes.

Accumulation is the cue that does not have that problem, because it is the one
thing rain cannot do. Rain leaves a wet deck; snow leaves a **city that has
changed shape**.

It is a third canvas per city layer, built only when `COLD`, painted in the same
pass that already paints the silhouette and the lights, and drawn at
`renderOrder = -30 + i*2 + .5` — after that layer's own silhouette so the caps
cover the rooflines they sit on, before its own lights, all of which are below a
roofline anyway. **Normal blending, never additive**: an additive cap is a light
source bolted to every roof in the city.

Two things had to be got right.

**Depth is set from the canvas, not from the building.** A physical snow depth
is under half a pixel on the far layer and simply does not exist. What saves it
is an accident of the layout: the four layers run 330/245/180/132 units out with
1024/1024/2048/2048-wide textures, and those two ratios very nearly cancel — a
canvas pixel lands within **1.2–2.4 screen pixels on every layer**. So a
constant in canvas units is close enough to a constant on screen, which is the
only place a viewer judges it. `capT = min(H*.019, t.w*.30)`, and the caps are
broken into a few chunks of different thickness because a rectangle of even
depth is a drawn line, and a drawn line along every roofline reads as an outline
pass rather than as weather.

**A ledge only holds a ledge's worth.** The projecting mechanical decks take the
snow too — three bands down each tower is what turns a white roofline into
depth — but at the roof's own thickness they came out as wide white bars at
three heights on every tower in the frame, which reads as ventilation louvres,
or as one more lit band in a skyline that already has two. Held to
`min(capT*.30, deck thickness*.9)` they read as ledges. The factor was .42 and
had to come down when `capT` grew by half: the deck bands rode along with the
roof caps and put the louvres straight back, four white bars down every tower.
Only the rooflines were meant to get heavier, so the factor is set to hold the
bands at the absolute thickness they already read correctly at.

The thirty-four low utility blocks between the glass and the skyline get the
same treatment as one extra `InstancedMesh` — the roof-marking planes again, at
full footprint and in white. They are the closest built thing to the window, and
a snowed city behind thirty bare rooftops reads as the weather stopping at the
middle distance. They are graded against the deck rather than the sky, because
unlike the painted city they sit inside the room's own lighting.

Everything fades on `snowLay`, not `snowK` — the same accumulation clock the
deck uses. A city that whitened on the weather clock while the deck below it
took another half minute would tear the two apart.

**The first pass of this was too quiet to do its job.** Built at
`capT = min(H*.012, t.w*.22)` with the city caps at `snowLay*(.42+daylight*.46)`
and the block roofs at `snowLay*(.46+daylight*.40)`, it was correct, measurable,
and still drew the same report back: *I cannot tell them apart.* Being able to
find an effect in a pixel diff is not the same as it reading at a glance, and
the second is the only one that was asked for. The constants now run
`min(H*.019, t.w*.30)`, `.58+daylight*.38` and `.62+daylight*.34`, and the deck
below whitens on `snowLay*.60` with its markings buried at `1-snowLay*.68`.
Those five numbers are the whole of how heavy the snow is; nothing else needs
touching to make it lighter again.

##### And the light it is not allowed to add

The curtain used to go both paler and denser in a blizzard, on the argument that
falling snow carries its own light. It does. It also made the window **brighter
when the weather turned**, which is backwards and is the second time this one
surface has been caught doing it. The `snowK` terms are gone: a cold storm and a
warm storm now drop exactly the same veil, `clamp(W.haze*2.2, 0, .58)`, measured
at .396 in both.

The caps had to clear the same bar, and they do. Measured by freezing every
moving source in the frame and toggling only the caps and the block roofs on the
same loaded page — the honest A/B, because the falling flakes put a 2.6–5.1
noise floor on the aperture crop and drown a real signal:

| | caps off | caps on | noise floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 21:00 blizzard | 46.69 | **46.51** | 0.11 |
| 13:00 blizzard | 68.67 | **67.72** | 3.71 |

Both slightly *negative*, and at night the effect is barely twice a noise floor
of 0.11. A cap is not free light: it covers lit windows below and replaces sky
above, and those two nearly cancel. The snow reads because it is **structured**
— a hard edge in the right place — not because it is bright.

At the heavier constants a blizzard aperture does sit above a clear one after
dark (42.15 against 35.87 at 21:00), and that is accumulation, not a glow: white
roofs against a near-black sky. The distinction worth holding is between *a
surface that has snow on it* and *a surface that has been made to emit* — the
first is the effect, the second is the bug that has now been found three times.

##### The white column on the mullion

The third one. The report was "there is still a white light on the glass and it
looks odd", with a screenshot of a pale vertical smear down the middle of the
window — and it was not the glass, the curtain, the reflection sheet or the
snow. It was the **window frame**, and it was being lit by the room.

`skyFill` is two point lights standing in for the sky arriving through the
opening. They stood just *inside* it, at `(16,19,-12)` and `(13,19,-27)`, with
the glass at `z=-30` and `x=22`. The second sat **2.6 units** from the vertical
mullion at `(14,16.5,-29.65)`, and a decay-2 point light at 2.6 units is not a
fill, it is a bulb screwed to the frame. Measured on an 11x120 patch of that
post, mean of 255:

| | before | after | unlit floor |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 13:00 storm | 157.7 | **46.2** | 32.9 |
| 08:00 cloudy | 153.9 | **56.5** | 47.1 |
| 21:00 blizzard | 98.5 | **44.7** | 41.8 |
| 17:00 clear | 177.7 | **82.8** | — |

The fix is not dimmer, it is **farther, and outside**. Sky does not light the
room-facing side of a window frame — it is behind that face — so moving the
sources beyond the glass, to `(24,19,-2)` and `(13,19,-36)`, drops the mullions
to the ambient floor *by geometry* rather than by a fudge, and leaves them free
to be lit by the sun when the sun is genuinely on them: 17:00 still reads 83, it
just no longer clips at 178.

`amb.intensity*900` becomes `*1400` to hold the room where it was. Measured at
four hours the room's mean moves by at most **+0.26/255**, and the gradient that
says the light comes from the window — the window-side crop brighter than the
far-side crop — keeps 16.3 of its 18.8 at sunset and 8.1 of its 10.2 at eight.
Moving them further still (16 and 24 units out) killed more of that gradient for
no further gain on the post.

##### What a cold visit switches off

- **Beads.** `wetTarget()` returns 0, which is the whole gate — the bead sheets
  are already `visible=false` below .02 and cost nothing from there.
- **Lightning**, in `wBlend`, by zeroing `W.flash` where `W` is assembled rather
  than at each of the four leashes that read it. Thundersnow is real and it is
  rare. The debug key still fires; it ignores the weather on purpose.
- **The hiss**, entirely. See below.

And what it switches on: `snowLay`, the same trick as `wet` with the constants
pushed a long way out — 26 s to settle, 52 s to go, because the ground is meant
to disagree with the sky and snow disagrees for longer than water does. It does
the opposite of rain to the deck in **both** halves: rain darkens the surface
and brightens the markings cut into it, snow pales the surface and **buries**
the markings. Measured at 21:00, `groundFX.base` goes `#4f4967` → `#888fa3`
while the lane materials go `.342` → `.153`; on the deck crop that is a mean
abs diff of 2.85 against clear, where the wet street — which ships — manages
1.59.

##### The room goes quiet

This is two multipliers and a filter corner, and it is the most effective thing
in the feature. There is no hiss at all, because hiss is water hitting hard
surfaces and snow does not hit; the body drops to 42% and its bandpass moves
from 480 Hz down to **190 Hz**, so what is left is wind against glass rather
than water on concrete. Measured on an `AnalyserNode` tapping `sMaster`:

| | rain path | snow path |
| --- | --- | --- |
| heaviest (storm / blizzard) | rms .01205, centroid 4314 Hz | rms **.00279**, centroid **2748 Hz** |
| moderate (rain / snow) | rms .00788 | rms **.00209** |
| hiss gain, heaviest | .0654 | **0** |

A blizzard is **77% quieter and an octave darker** than the storm it is
otherwise identical to. Not silent, and deliberately: a blizzard has a low roar,
light snow and heavy snow have to stay distinguishable from each other, and the
one state a visitor must be able to tell apart from "muted" is "on, and the
weather is quiet". At rms .00279 against a room tone of .00139 it sits at twice
the floor — audible, and nowhere near rain.

##### The harness bug that nearly shipped a wrong conclusion

The first audio measurement said snow was exactly as loud as rain. It was not:
`Page.navigate` to a URL differing only in its `#hash` is a **same-document
navigation**, so the "cold" build was the warm page with a different fragment
and `COLD` was 0 in both runs. `Page.reload(ignoreCache=True)` after setting the
hash is the fix, and it is the second time this trap has cost an hour. Any probe
that reads a value decided at *load* has to force a real document load.

#### Weather is not on the click, deliberately

The obvious hook is to roll the weather when someone opens a project. Three
things are wrong with it, and the third is the one that settles it.

- **`applyTime` is a pure function of the hour and reads as one.** The light
  moves because time passed, and the scrub bar lets anyone prove it. Weather
  hung off a click has no cause the room can show: the same cover opens sun one
  time and a storm the next, and neither the room nor the visitor can say why.
  That reads as a bug, not as weather.
- **A storm behind an open panel is noise on the text.** Lightning especially.
- **It would fire where nobody can see it.** The main grid is at x=-18 and the
  glass starts at x=6, so `focusOn` parks the camera facing the far end of the
  wall from the window, and `placePanel` covers what is left. The one moment a
  click could trigger weather is the one moment the window is out of frame.

#### Lightning

The only part of the weather allowed to be sudden, and therefore the only part
carrying a leash. Three rules, none negotiable: nothing under
`prefers-reduced-motion`; nothing while `focus_` or `hudCard` is set, because a
flash behind a paragraph is a flash on the paragraph; and a hard floor between
strikes. WCAG 2.3.1 counts flashes per second and a storm is precisely the
effect that likes to exceed three — the floor is a separate check rather than a
bigger random interval, because a random interval is a tendency and this needs
to be a guarantee.

**That floor is `fDur + 400`, derived, not a constant.** It has to outlast the
strike — two overlapping strikes are two flashes and the "one strike is one
flash" argument below stops covering them — and `fDur` is a roll between 2.3 and
6.8 seconds, so no single number is both safe at the top of that range and not
absurd at the bottom. The `fDur` read at that moment is still the *previous*
strike's, which is exactly the one that has to have finished. It went 900 →
1400 → derived as the lifetime grew; a constant was going to be wrong again.

**A strike is not a blink.** The first version ramped `flashK` linearly to zero
over 240ms and then squared it in `applyTime` — a camera flash. Real lightning
is a stepped leader, a return stroke and one to four restrikes spread over a few
hundred milliseconds, and what the eye receives is a flickering glow that fades.
`newStrike()` builds that: an envelope of `pow(1-t/dur, 2.2)` for the fast rise
and the long tail, with 1–4 stroke pulses modulating it.

**The strokes modulate the envelope between .5 and 1; they never restart it.**
That is the realism and the accessibility argument at the same time. A glow that
dips to half and recovers is not a second flash — WCAG 2.3.1 needs a *pair of
opposing* luminance changes — so a four-stroke strike is one flash with texture
on it rather than four flashes inside a second. Simulated over 600 strikes: zero
opposing swings above 10% of peak inside a strike, and the 1400ms floor caps the
rate at 0.71/sec against a limit of 3.

**Lifetime is its own roll.** `fDur` is `(3200 + random()*3600) * (1 - fNear*.30)`
— a 3.2–6.8s base that distance only *leans*, rather than a value distance
dictates outright. It was `1150 - fNear*470` at first, which meant two strikes at
the same distance always lasted exactly the same time. Measured across 3000
sampled strikes: lifetime 2.3–6.8s with a median of 4.6s, and time visible above
10% of peak went 530ms → 2409ms, **4.5x longer**.

**Stroke timing scales with the lifetime** (`sc = fDur/1000`), covering both the
gaps between strokes and the width of each pulse. Left absolute at 45–180ms
apart with a 190ms pulse, the entire multi-stroke flicker finished inside the
first tenth of a five-second envelope and the other 90% was a plain smooth fade
— the strokes would have been present in the code and unreadable on screen.

**Distance is the other roll.** `fNear` is `random()*random()`, skewed far, and
it sets peak, leans duration, and decides how much of the strike reaches indoors — `fr` scales only the room-side terms, so a horizon
strike lights the sky and barely touches the desk while a near one floods both.
`fPeak` floors at .45: dropping the square in `applyTime` already lowered the
mid-envelope, and a far strike peaking at .28 on top of that is a grey shrug
rather than distant lightning.

That square had to go with this change. The envelope already carries `pow 2.2`;
squaring it again made `pow 4.4`, which is precisely the 240ms snap the rewrite
existed to remove. **The shape lives in `stepFlash` and nowhere else.**

`flashK` advances *before* any of the gates, so a strike already in flight when
a panel opens fades out cleanly instead of freezing at peak behind it. The
strike is applied last in `applyTime`, on top of everything the hour decided, so
it adds and never accumulates.

Measured at 1200x800, pinned `#w=storm&t=20.6`, each against an otherwise
identical frame with the flash forced off. `max` is useless here — a handful of
emissive pixels saturate at every level — so these are mean delta and the share
of the frame that moved at all:

| | peak | at 300ms | envelope |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| the original single shape | mean 22.6, 67% of frame | gone | 164ms |
| near strike (`fNear` .95) | mean 16.6, 62% of frame | mean 3.6, 18% | 703ms |
| mid strike (`fNear` .45) | mean 8.6, 46% of frame | mean 2.4, 9% | 939ms |

Those envelope figures predate the lifetime change; the brightness columns still
hold, the durations are now 4.5x longer. A live sample of one strike
(`fDur` 5161ms, `fNear` .02) read 0.41 → 0.37 → 0.22 → 0.13 → 0.07 across the
first four seconds — a long smooth fade rather than a snap.

Note when measuring this headless: `tick` clamps `dt` to 50ms, so under
swiftshader at ~5fps the envelope advances at roughly a quarter of wall-clock
speed. The clamp is correct — it stops a backgrounded tab jumping the animation
— but it makes wall-clock sampling of a headless capture read short.

A near strike is therefore about as bright as the old one and lasts four times
as long; a distant one is softer, slower and mostly outside the glass. Clear
against storm at noon differs across 80% of the frame.

**Interval: `FLASH_GAP()` is a flat 10–20s.** An earlier version clustered them —
a fifth of strikes as a quick follow-up from the same cell, the rest 7–26s
apart — on the argument that a flat interval reads as a metronome. It was
replaced on request with the flat range, which is what the room now ships.
Measured over 4000 simulated ten-minute storms, 87% of observed gaps land inside
the 10–20s band with a median of 15.5s; the remainder are storms ending and
restarting, not the interval drifting.

**`1` fires a strike on demand.** Waiting out a weather cycle to look at a 700ms
effect is not a workflow, and the alternative — a throwaway copy with the
envelope pinned — can only produce stills of it. The key ignores the weather,
which is the point of it, and ignores the open-panel gate, because someone
pressing it is asking to see the flash and knows where it will land. It does not
ignore the other two: `prefers-reduced-motion` still means no, and the 1400ms
floor still holds. That floor is load-bearing here rather than incidental — a
held key auto-repeats around thirty times a second, and without it this one line
would be a strobe generator sitting on a public page. Verified in-browser: 20
keydowns dispatched back to back leave the first strike's envelope running
untouched.

#### Reduced motion

`stepWeather` is called with `dt=0`, which freezes the blend at whatever state
the visit arrived on. Forcing clear would have been a decision the preference
never asked for — a still storm is a legitimate sky. Lightning is off entirely.

### Sound

**It lives in `demos/room/sound.js`, and it is the only part of the room that
does.** It was picked to be first for being the least entangled thing in the
file: 275 lines that read exactly two values from the rest of the room — the
weather object `W`, and whether this is a cold visit — and own everything else
about themselves, including the mute button, the volume slider, the storage and
the arming on first gesture. `W` is a `const` object mutated in place, so one
reference stays live; `windLean` is reassigned every frame, so it goes in as a
`tick()` argument instead. The room calls three things: `sound.tick(now,
windLean)`, `sound.duck(on)` and `sound.thunder(near, peak)`.

That is the bar for anything lifted next. Not "this section is long" — every
section is long — but "this piece reads less from the room than it gives back".

Everything is synthesised. The room is one HTML file whose textures are drawn
into canvases at load, and the same discipline says the audio is generated too —
it turns out rain and thunder are the two easiest things in the world to build
out of filtered noise. The section adds **no asset, no request and no decode**,
which matters more than usual on a page a recruiter opens once, on someone
else's wifi, and closes.

The bed is not an independent thing that happens to play during rain. Like every
visual weather term it is a function of `W`, computed from the same numbers in
the same frame, so the sound cannot disagree with the window about how hard it
is raining. `windLean` drives the bed's brightness, so the gusts you can see are
the gusts you can hear.

**Autoplay is a constraint, not a preference.** No browser starts audio before a
real gesture, so "on by default" can only ever mean *armed, and running from the
first interaction*. This room needs a drag to look around, so that arrives within
seconds. The choice is remembered in `localStorage`, and a visitor who has muted
gets **no `AudioContext` at all** on their next visit — not a suspended one,
none. Muting mid-session ramps the master down over 350ms and then suspends the
context, so a muted tab is not holding an audio thread open.

#### The bed

One noise buffer, played by **two sources at incommensurable playback rates**. A
looped noise buffer has no click at its seam — one random sample follows another,
which is what noise is — but it does have a *period*, and four seconds is short
enough to hear the same shower come round again. Two loops beating against each
other push that period past anyone's patience.

Two bands, and they arrive at different times: a **body** bandpassed at 480Hz,
and a **hiss** for rain hitting glass, which only really exists once it is coming
down hard. Both were measured with an `AnalyserNode` tapped onto the master:

| | body gain | hiss gain | RMS | spectral centroid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| storm | .208 | .108 | .0204 | 4366 Hz |
| rain | .113 | .041 | .0093 | 4232 Hz |
| cloudy | .024 | .003 | .0017 | 4892 Hz |
| clear | 0 | 0 | .0013 | 690 Hz |

The table is the level *before* the knee below; heavy rain now measures .0117.

Two numbers in that table were bugs found by measuring:

- **The hiss needs a ceiling as much as a floor.** A lone highpass passes
  everything to Nyquist, and a storm measured a centroid of **9.1kHz** — that is
  not rain, it is static. Rain sits around 2–4kHz because a drop on glass is a
  small impact. Adding a lowpass helped but only reached 6.1kHz: one biquad is
  12dB/octave, which two octaves above the corner is still not a ceiling. Two
  cascaded stages at 5600Hz brought it to 4366Hz. Raising Q instead would have
  put a resonant peak exactly where the brightness already was.
- **The hiss exponent was `r²` and made rain-to-storm a 5.3x loudness step.** A
  portfolio should not startle anyone; the weather fade is 17 seconds and the ear
  should have to work slightly to notice it has been crossed. At `r^1.6` the step
  is 2.2x.

Clear weather is not silence — it is the **room tone**, a lowpassed whisper at
690Hz that you notice only when it is gone. Digital silence is what a broken
audio system sounds like.

#### The knee on the loud end

Heavy rain was too loud. Overcast and the room tone were not, and scaling the
whole bed would have taken those down for a complaint nobody made — so it is a
soft knee, `1 - .40 * r^1.2`, not a trim:

| | knee | measured |
| --- | --- | --- |
| storm | .605 | −42% |
| rain | .809 | −19% |
| overcast | .970 | −3% |

The dynamic range between weathers narrows instead of the whole thing getting
quieter, which is also what a limiter would have done to it. Applied to both
bands equally, so the spectral centroid does not move — measured 4366Hz before,
4444Hz after. A knee on only one band would have recoloured the rain while
turning it down.

#### Volume

The mute button is the anchor; the slider is a popup **anchored to it, not laid
out beside it.** The clock bar is centred with `translateX(-50%)`, so a control
that changes the bar's width on hover slides the whole bar sideways under the
cursor as you reach for it.

`#vol` is the hit area and carries no chrome; `.vi` inside it carries the
chrome. The gap between button and panel therefore lives *inside* the hoverable
box, so the pointer never crosses dead space on the way up. Verified by walking
a synthetic cursor from the button centre to the slider centre in 4px steps:
the panel stays `visible` for every one of them.

It opens on `:focus-within` as well as `:hover`, which is the entire keyboard
story — and a subtle one. A `visibility:hidden` subtree **cannot be focused**,
so the slider is not directly reachable; it is reachable by tabbing from the
button, whose focus is what reveals it. Traced: focus `#snd` → popup visible →
Tab → `#volR` → six ArrowRights take 50% to 56% with the master gain following
exactly, and the room's own arrow handler stands down because it already bails
on `e.target.closest('input,button,a,…')`.

**Not on touch.** There is no hover to open it, the clock bar is already
wrapping at that width, and a phone has hardware volume keys six inches from the
thumb that are better than anything this could be. Mute stays on every device.

Two states can contradict each other — a mute flag and a volume — so one of them
wins in one place: a slider at zero **is** muted, whatever the flag says, and
unmuting from zero bumps the volume to 30% because a button that does nothing
visible is a broken button. The slider ramps over 50ms rather than the button's
350ms: the slider *is* the ramp, and a fade chasing the thumb lags it visibly.
Not zero, because a step change on a gain node is a click.

Suspending is worth doing and worth delaying. A context left running at gain
zero still holds an audio thread, but a slider dragged *through* zero on its way
somewhere else must not tear the graph down and rebuild it — so the teardown
waits 1.5s to see whether the silence was meant.

**`SND_TRIM = .5`** sits under all of it — the room was mixed loud, and the fix
is half the bus. It is a trim rather than a lower default for one reason:
`SND.vol` is read from `localStorage` on the way in, so lowering the default
would only ever reach a first-time visitor. Anyone who has touched the slider
carries their own number and would never see the change. Applied at both places
the master gain is written — the arm/mute fade and the slider's 50ms
`setTargetAtTime` — so there is no path that escapes it. Verified on an
`AnalyserNode`: at the default slider the master gain reads .2750 where it used
to read .5500, and the blizzard bed measures rms .00136 against .00279 before,
which is a ratio of .49.

#### Thunder

The one moment worth the whole section, and nearly free: `fNear` already exists
because the flash needed to know how far away it was. Sound covers a kilometre in
three seconds, so **the delay is the distance** — the same number that decides
whether the room lights up decides how long you wait to hear about it.

| `fNear` | onset, predicted | onset, measured | peak (post-master) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| .95 | 725ms | 860ms | .085 |
| .50 | 4550ms | 4721ms | .043 |
| .06 | 8290ms | 8435ms | .021 |

The overshoot is the analyser window plus the 25ms sampling grid.

**The rolls are the point of the envelope.** Thunder is not a fade; it is a sound
that keeps arriving, because it is one discharge heard along a path kilometres
long with every part of it at a different distance. A smooth decay reads as a
sound effect. Traced, a near strike is a 1.3s crack that matches its `dur` of
1.34s exactly, and a far one runs 5.6s and **peaks again at 11.6s louder than it
first arrived at 9.0s** — which is what distant thunder actually does.

**Distance is paid for twice and the second one is easy to miss.** The gain term
takes it once; the lowpass takes it again, because a narrower band carries less
energy for the same gain. Measured, a far strike first came out at .0117
post-master against a storm bed sitting at .0204 — the rumble was quieter than
the rain that caused it. Making the corner linear rather than squared, and
leaning the gain term back toward the far end, doubled it. Near strikes were
never the ones in trouble and are unchanged.

`sndThunder` is called from `newStrike`, not from `stepFlash`, so there is one
place a strike can be born and the debug key `1` gets its thunder too.

#### Ducking

A showreel clip carries its own audio and the bed would fight it. `#hud.playing`
already existed, so the three sites that toggle it duck the bed to .16 — ducked,
not paused, because cutting the bed dead and bringing it back is more noticeable
than leaving it under.

### Camera

The pivot sits inside the room and yaw/pitch/distance are clamped so the orbit
can never push through a wall or the ceiling — cheaper and steadier than
collision, and it keeps framing on the two walls that matter.

`fit()` handles aspect. A fixed 48° vertical FOV is a landscape assumption:
held upright at 48° the horizontal field collapses to about 23° and the room
loses the window, the sofa and its whole right-hand half. Below 16:10 the
vertical FOV opens instead so the horizontal stays roughly constant, capped at
86° before wide-angle distortion becomes the thing you notice. **This is
already solved — do not "fix" portrait framing by changing the FOV.**

### Performance decisions

- DPR clamped to 2 with MSAA on. A previous 1.2x-without-MSAA setting threw
  away most of an iPhone 13 Pro's panel before presenting the frame.
- Anisotropic filtering (up to 8x) on screen and image textures; **nearest**
  sampling on the packed palette atlases, where linear filtering bleeds colour
  between adjacent UV islands.
- Near plane at 1.0, not 0.4 — the old value spent most of the depth buffer on
  space the camera can never enter.
- Occlusion fading (`occCand`, `occUpdate`) so props between camera and target
  do not block a focused surface.

### Failure paths

- **WebGL missing** → `#nogl` card with a link to the 2D archive.
- **Boot exception** → caught before the module runs, printed into the loading
  card rather than leaving a black screen.
- **A model never arrives** → `setTimeout(() => modelsIn = true, 8000)`. Nothing
  behind the loading screen depends on the props, and a corrupt `.glb` once
  left the room permanently behind a Loading card.

### QA harness

`#qa=1` runs `runRoomQA()` in the real browser and prints a table. Per screen it
checks: projected bounds agree with the DOM rect, no blurring transform, the
responsive layout matches what the container query should have produced, the
three header controls are hit-testable via `elementFromPoint`, links exist and
are clickable, and a real `wheel` event scrolls the real container. Then it
checks paging does not move the camera, `View in room` does, and both close
paths work.

It deliberately does not open external links.

**Current status: 11/11**, driven over CDP at 1440x900 and 1280x720. It was
13 before the desk pass below: three of the eleven panels were retired, and
each panel is one row. The eleventh row is Wall cover mapping, which is not a
panel — see the wall section. The centre monitor has not had a row since it stopped opening
one.

Driving it needs real CDP, not `--virtual-time-budget`. Under virtual time the

that last ran this is a stdlib-only socket WebSocket, about 80 lines — attach to
`/json`, `Runtime.evaluate` a poll on `#qa`'s `innerHTML`, read the table out
of the DOM. macOS has no `timeout(1)`, and a headless Edge that is killed
mid-run leaves a lock in its `--user-data-dir` that makes the next launch exit
instantly with an empty capture; use a fresh profile directory.

**The long-standing `Showreel · Controls` failure was real, and it is fixed.**
It had been written off here twice as a harness artefact. It was not. Measured
inside the sweep, `#gh` and `#gb` sat 46px above the `#glass` box that contains
them, so the probe at each header button's centre landed on the panel's own top
edge instead. The cause was `glass.scrollTop = 26.63`, which is exactly 46px
once the 1.765 panel zoom is applied: **`overflow:hidden` still makes an element
a scroll container**, so the harness's `scrollIntoView` on a body link scrolled
the panel *frame* and slid the entire header — title, paging, Close — up out of
the clip, with no scrollbar to bring it back.

The fix is `overflow:clip` on `#glass` (`overflow:hidden` kept ahead of it for
browsers that lack `clip`), which creates no scroll container at all and leaves
`#gb` as the only thing a focus or a `scrollIntoView` can scroll. A
`glass.scrollTop = 0` in `renderPanelContent` covers the fallback path.

Today only the harness reaches this — `spotlightBuild` deliberately computes
`panelBody.scrollTop` by hand rather than calling `scrollIntoView`, and the Tab
handler's focus moves are absorbed by `#gb`. So it was a latent bug rather than
one visitors were hitting, but any future `scrollIntoView` or unguarded
`focus()` inside the panel body would have made the Close button unreachable.
The fix removes the whole class.

### The debug readout

Five taps inside a 100x100 square in the bottom-left corner opens `#dbg`. It
reports fps, draw calls and triangles, the camera as a pasteable `#cam=` string,
the hour as `#t=`, whatever prop is under the pointer, and — the reason it
exists — the **world point under the pointer, named by the surface it landed
on**: `back wall  x -13.5  y 28.2`. Placing anything in this room means choosing
coordinates, and choosing them off a screenshot is guesswork. **Copy for Claude**
puts all of it on the clipboard as one line.

**Zones** paints numbered patches onto the surfaces worth talking about, and
the POINT line names the zone the pointer is in. The whole reason the readout
exists is to answer *which wall, and where on it*; a number burned onto the
wall itself is a shorter answer than a coordinate. The ids match the table in
this document, so retire one rather than reuse it — renumbering silently is
worse than leaving a gap. The patches draw with `depthTest` off so they stay
legible through whatever stands in front of them, and their `raycast` is
stubbed out, so they label a surface without ever becoming a pick target.

Patches on one surface share a run and never touch. The four on the left wall
were drawn from three different runs along it — the poster's, a `z=-13` pair,
and the column's — and at the raking angle that wall is always seen at, three
staggered runs read as one pile of overlapping cards; 7 genuinely did overlap 8.
6 and 7 now share the poster's exact run, so 6/5/7 stack as one column and 8
stands 1.25 clear of its forward edge. The vertical gaps are measured off the
poster's two lit rails rather than the poster face, so no patch sits on a strip
of magenta light. Touching edges are as bad as overlap here: at a grazing view
two patches sharing a border cannot be told from two patches on top of each
other, so leave a gap wide enough to survive the foreshortening.

Which zones are worth anything was settled by measurement, not by looking:
sweep the quad across the whole yaw/pitch/zoom space and clip its projected
polygon to the viewport. **1, 2 and 8 are the good ones** — all three sit near
32 degrees off square, which is what the main grid itself manages. **9 works
but only at the far-left end of the drag**, and on a 1440-wide window half of
it still hangs off the right edge; it needs 1920 to be fully framed. It also
moved a unit forward, to `z=3.8`: at `2.8` its back edge sat 0.95 from where the
wall becomes glass at `z=-2`, close enough to the mullion that the patch read as
hanging off the window rather than sitting on the wall. That costs a little of
the framing it was already short of, and is worth it. 3 and 7 are crossed by the
monitors and the shelf, 4 sits behind the corner cabinet, 5 is the poster, 6 is
a thin strip that suits a row of small frames.

The first pass of that measurement got 8 and 9 wrong, and it is worth knowing
why: one probe quad per wall, at z=3 and z=6, both too far forward, and the
conclusion drawn was that the walls were unusable rather than that the samples
were badly placed. Sampling a surface at one point and generalising to the
surface is the mistake to watch for here.

Two decisions in it are load-bearing:

**No element sits in the corner.** A 100x100 div with pointer events there would
be a dead spot in the orbit drag, and the bottom-left corner is prime dragging
real estate. The counter reads the pointer position off a capture-phase
`pointerup` on `window` instead, and requires the gesture to have moved less
than 10px so a drag that happens to end there does not count. As a side effect
it also works on a phone, where `#clock` covers the corner outright.

**The raycast is the structural shell only, at 8 Hz, and only while open.**
Raycasting the whole scene would hit the rain sheets, the glass and the additive
light shafts long before it reached a wall, and report a normal belonging to
none of them; the target list is filtered to meshes using the room's own wall,
floor, ceiling, frame and metal materials. A dev tool that costs frames is a dev
tool that lies about fps.

## 3. Accessibility

Not an afterthought here, which is unusual for a 3D portfolio.

- The canvas carries `role="img"` with a label and a described-by pointing at
  an off-screen instructions paragraph.
- **`☰ Contents`** is the keyboard and screen-reader route into every surface.
  Nothing in the room is reachable *only* by aiming at pixels.
- The panel is a real `role="dialog" aria-modal="true"` with a Tab focus trap,
  Escape to close, and focus returned to whatever opened it.
- `←`/`→` page panel content; without a panel open they nudge the camera.
- Opening the panel makes the canvas and the room UI `inert`.
- `prefers-reduced-motion` collapses animation and transition durations.
- 44px minimum touch targets under `(pointer: coarse)`.
- Safe-area insets (`env(safe-area-inset-*)`) on every edge-anchored element.

## 4. Baked assets

What ships under `demos/` is largely generated rather than hand-made. Each
tool is idempotent; re-run it when the source it reads has changed. The site is
static with no build step, so every output below is a file in the repository.

| File | Tool | Rebuild when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the `links` and `tags` of `demos/portfolio-data.js` | `tools/links.py` | `build_links.txt` changes |
| `wall.jpg`, `wall-left.jpg`, `wall-right.jpg`, `wall-layout.js` | `tools/wallsheet.py` | `build_layout.txt`, a title, a thumbnail, or a category colour in `CAT` changes |
| `badge.jpg`, `avatar.jpg` | `tools/badge.py` | the photo or the ID details change |
| `og.jpg` | `tools/ogimage.py` | the room's look changes — this is the link preview |
| `cybercity.png`, `scifi.png` | `tools/atlastint.py` | never, unless a new Synty pack is added |
| `clips/*.mp4` | `tools/clips.py` | a wall hero changes, or its video is re-cut |
| `sofacat.glb` | `tools/sculptglb.py cat` | never, unless the cat is replaced |
| `chairplush.glb` | `tools/sculptglb.py plush` | never, unless the penguin is replaced |

`tools/portfolio.py` is not in the table because it is not a bake step: it is
what every other tool reads the project data, the category table and the slug
rule through, so there is one implementation of each rather than one per tool.

`tools/sculptglb.py` takes both Sketchfab sculpts down to something a page can
carry — the cat from 6.6 MB to 196 K, the penguin from 3.6 MB to 25 K:

    python3 tools/sculptglb.py cat   SRC.glb demos/models/sofacat.glb
    python3 tools/sculptglb.py plush SRC.glb demos/models/chairplush.glb

Any profile setting can be overridden for a sweep, which is how the cat's gamma
and contrast were picked:

    python3 tools/sculptglb.py cat SRC.glb out.glb gamma=0.6 contrast=1.5

It reaches meshoptimizer through `npx --yes @gltf-transform/cli@4`, so there is
nothing to install but there is a network fetch at build time. It is a build
tool — nothing in the room fetches anything from a third party at runtime. The
reasoning behind each step is in its docstring; the short version is in
[The cat on the sofa](#the-cat-on-the-sofa).

`tools/fbx2glb.py` converts a Synty `.fbx` into the `.glb` the room loads:

    python3 tools/fbx2glb.py SRC.fbx demos/models/OUT.glb ATLAS.png [geo indices]

Everything it writes lands in one primitive under one material, which is only
correct while the prop draws from a single atlas. Several Synty props do not —
`SM_Prop_Holo_Planter_01` is a pot on the pack atlas plus a tree on its own
foliage sheet — and merged, the second texture's UVs sample the atlas instead,
which renders as the whole sheet smeared across the mesh. Run it with no fourth
argument first: it prints each Geometry node's vertex count and UV range, and a
node reaching 0..1 is the one to leave out. `demos/models/planter.glb` is that
model's node 0 on its own.

A model added to `demos/models/` also has to be added to `USES` in
`tools/atlastint.py` and the tool re-run, or the swatches only that model
samples keep the pack's construction-site yellow.

`tools/clips.py` needs `yt-dlp` and `ffmpeg`. It is idempotent — an existing
clip is kept unless `--force` — and `--cache DIR` keeps the downloads, which is
what makes re-picking an offset cheap. Three things in it are per-title and were
chosen by looking at sampled frames, not guessed: `STARTS`, because the default
offset lands on a pair of Unity editor windows in the netcode captures, on the
parental disclaimer in the Kinder recording, and on the avatar dressing room in
the AAF one; `CROPS`, because nine of the sources frame the game inside
something else; and `CRFS`, for the three sources that are four seconds of
continuous motion and blow past 200 KB at the default quality.

Two things about `CROPS` are worth keeping. Four of its rects are windowed Unity
editor captures where most of the frame is title bars and a Console. The other
five are screen recordings of Edge with the WebGL build running in a tab — a URL
bar, browser chrome and a field of page white around a 400x600 canvas. Uncropped
they are a screenshot of a browser, which is what `SKIP` had measured them as;
cropped to the canvas they are the game. The rects were found by taking the
standard deviation of six frames and reading off the region that actually moves,
since page white does not move and neither does chrome.

**The download picks its rendition by width, not height, and that is not a
detail.** The output is a 640x360 landscape crop, so width is the axis that
survives; the original `height<=720` filter capped a 1080x2400 phone capture at
its 288x640 rendition, which was then upscaled 2.2x to fill the frame and read
as a smear. `-S +width` takes the *smallest* rendition clearing the bar rather
than the biggest, which is what keeps a 16:9 source landing on the same 1280x720
it always did — and that is what keeps the `CROPS` pixel rects valid. A cover with no clip keeps its poster, which is what the
readout shows before any clip loads anyway. Nothing gets pasted anywhere now:
which titles it works on comes from `build_layout.txt`, and re-running
`tools/wallsheet.py` afterwards regenerates the room's `CLIPS` set from whatever
ended up on disk.

`SKIP` is the fourth, and it is now a record rather than a gate. It holds the
twelve sources that were baked, looked at and rejected, each with the measured
reason — but every one of them has since been cut anyway, under `--all
--skipped`, because a clip outlives the wall it was cut for and re-downloading
one to promote a build later is work that does not need doing twice. `--all`
takes the whole archive instead of the wall; `--skipped` says to bake past the
verdicts. **The consequence is the thing to keep in mind:** `CLIPS` is
enumerated from what is on disk, so a rejected preview is now live the moment
its build reaches a wall, with no second decision. That costs nothing while the
build sits on a prop — props do not hover, and nothing fetches a clip that is
never hovered — but it means `SKIP` no longer protects the readout. If one of
those previews turns up on a wall and looks as bad as it was measured to look,
the fix is to take the build off the wall or delete the file, not to edit `SKIP`.

Three builds have no clip and cannot get one: `Topdown Sword`, `Game3d Racing`
and `Game2d Black Ops` carry no YouTube link in `portfolio-data.js`, so there is
no source to cut. That is also why the first two of them were kept off the wall.

`og.jpg` exists because scrapers want 1200×630 at an absolute URL and will not
run JavaScript to find out what a page looks like. The preview for a portfolio
whose whole point is a rendered room has to be a rendered frame, baked ahead of
time. `tools/ogimage.py` composes it from `tools/og-source.png`, a headless
capture whose exact command is in the script's docstring.

### Asset hygiene

Verified: all 66 thumbnails referenced by `portfolio-data.js` exist in
`images/thumb/`, and no thumbnail in that directory is unreferenced.

Note that `portfolio-data.js` stores paths as `../images/name.jpg`, but both
pages route every path through a `thumb()` helper that rewrites `../images/`
to `../images/thumb/`. Full-size images are not shipped and nothing asks for
them. The `info` path is tried first, with `intro` as an `onerror` fallback.

The two `images/*.webp` files are the exception — they are full-size and loaded
directly, because they are the readout card and the monitor lockup rather than
project art. `duyon.webp` is 79K at 871x871, which is comfortably over 2x for
the 516px the readout gives it. Two things were done to it and both matter if
it is ever replaced:

- **The internal team name was painted out.** The source card names the team
  inside Athena. A team name at a game studio often maps to an unannounced
  project, and the card itself may only have been circulated internally, so it
  is not something a public portfolio should republish on the studio's behalf.
  It sat on flat white card stock at x 183-383, y 192-218, well clear of the
  circuit flourish at x 535+, so it was erased rather than cropped — the
  banner, the frame and the mascot all survive.
- **It was re-encoded at q88**, down from 459K. The original was near-lossless
  for an image that is never shown above 516 CSS px; the photo region measures
  36.6 dB PSNR against the edited source.

## 7. Running it

The pages use ES modules, so they need a server; opening the file directly will
not work.

```sh
python3 tools/serve.py
# → http://127.0.0.1:8000/
```

That bakes once at startup, then watches `build_layout.txt`, `build_links.txt`,
`portfolio-data.js` and `portfolio-shared.js`: saving any of them re-runs
`links.py` and then `wallsheet.py` and reloads the open page, so editing the
layout or a link is the whole gesture. The order is load-bearing — `links.py`
writes `portfolio-data.js` and `wallsheet.py` hashes it into the `?v=`. A layout that does not parse leaves the last good sheets in place and
prints why, rather than reloading you into a broken room. It serves with
`Cache-Control: no-store`, because the `?v=` that is right for the deployed
site is exactly wrong here — a re-bake that hashes the same would be served
from cache and look like the save did nothing.

Nothing it does ships. For a plain static server with no watching:

```sh
python3 -m http.server 8000 --bind 127.0.0.1
```

To test on a phone on the same trusted Wi-Fi, bind to the LAN and open
`http://<PC-LAN-IP>:8000/`:

```sh
python3 tools/serve.py --bind 0.0.0.0
```

Stop with `Ctrl+C` when testing is finished.

Baking the wall by hand, reading what the tool says, and committing the
result so it reaches Pages: `doc_build_layout.md`.

### Checks

Three, all fast, none needing anything installed, all run in CI on every push
(`.github/workflows/check.yml`):

```sh
python3 tools/links.py --check       # portfolio-data.js matches build_links.txt
python3 tools/wallsheet.py --check   # the wall map matches build_layout.txt
python3 tools/jscheck.py             # every shipped script parses (wants node)
```

The first two are the ones that matter, and in that order: `links.py` writes
`portfolio-data.js` and `wallsheet.py` hashes it into the `?v=` both pages load
it at, so running them the other way round reports a stale link block as a wall
problem -- the wrong place to go looking. Together they catch a `.txt` edited
without re-baking, which otherwise surfaces not as an error but as the site
simply not changing: a button that does not appear, a wall that does not move.

The room's own in-browser harness is `#qa=1` -- see **QA harness** in § 2.

### Changing what the room shows

What hangs on which wall, and in what order, is `build_layout.txt` at the repo
root: a heading per wall or prop, then the builds under it by id. Edit it, then
`python3 tools/wallsheet.py` bakes the sheets and regenerates the map the room
reads -- both come out of that one run. **`doc_build_layout.md`** is the guide:
the format, the `auto` rules the props can take, how to read the bake output.

Which links a build carries -- the WebGL build, the Play Store page, the video,
the source -- is `build_links.txt`, a block per build by id with four slots and
`-` for one it does not have. Edit it, then `python3 tools/links.py` writes
those links into `demos/portfolio-data.js`, then `python3 tools/wallsheet.py`
for the `?v=`. **`doc_build_links.md`** is the guide.

Every one of those outputs is a file in the repository rather than something
regenerated on deploy, because the site is static and Pages runs no Python.

### Headless capture

Useful for regressions and for re-shooting `og-source.png`:

```sh
"/Applications/Microsoft Edge.app/Contents/MacOS/Microsoft Edge" \
  --headless=new --disable-gpu-sandbox --enable-unsafe-swiftshader \
  --use-gl=angle --use-angle=swiftshader --window-size=1600,900 \
  --virtual-time-budget=26000 --screenshot=shot.png \
  "http://127.0.0.1:8000/room-3d.html#t=20.6"
```

Two caveats. This renders through SwiftShader, so colour, antialiasing and
timing differ from a real GPU. And `--virtual-time-budget` frequently captures
mid-transition — a screenshot showing the boot overlay still faded over a fully
rendered room is a capture artefact, not a bug. For anything layout-sensitive,
drive the browser over CDP with `Emulation.setDeviceMetricsOverride` and wait on
real state instead.

### Mobile display

The room requests browser fullscreen on the first canvas gesture where the
Fullscreen API is available. On iPhone, use **Share → Add to Home Screen**;
launching that icon uses the manifest's fullscreen display mode and safe-area
layout. A normal Safari tab keeps Safari's own chrome by design.

**Orientation is asked for, never taken.** Two APIs would take it —
`screen.orientation.lock('landscape')` behind fullscreen, and the manifest's
`orientation` field for an installed PWA — and both were built and then removed
on purpose. A lock is not a rotation: it pins the room landscape, so a visitor
who turns the phone back is ignored, and it only works on Android, so the two
platforms would diverge for a reason nobody outside this file can see. What is
shipped instead is `#rotate`, a dismissible card shown in portrait on a device
that can actually be turned, and after that every platform simply follows the
device. The manifest stays at `"orientation": "any"`. **Do not reintroduce the
lock without also having an answer for how the visitor gets back out of it.**

**The card's gate is `(pointer:coarse)`, not `MOBILE_GPU` / `.touch`, and the
difference is not cosmetic.** `MOBILE_GPU` is `pointer:coarse` *or* a short
side under 600px, so a narrow desktop window publishes `.touch` as well — this
is deliberate and documented above, but it means a card keyed off `.touch`
tells someone at a 520x900 desktop window to turn a device they are not
holding, when the answer there is to widen the window. Measured: that window
reports `pointer:coarse` false and `html.touch` true, which is exactly the gap.
The heading says "device" and not "phone" for the neighbouring reason — a
tablet held upright is coarse-pointered, in portrait, and gets the card too.

Because it covers the viewport and swallows every tap, the card is modal in
fact, so `aria-modal` is honest only if the room goes inert behind it —
`showRotate` sets `inert` on `#cv` and `#ui` and moves focus to the button,
`dismissRotate` puts all of it back. That is the same contract `#glass`
already keeps; a dialog that claims modality without it lets a screen reader
walk into a room the pointer cannot reach.

The compat facts behind that, since they are the first thing anyone re-checks:
**WebKit ships no `ScreenOrientation.lock` anywhere** — not iPhone, not iPad,
not desktop Safari (`safari: false` in MDN's compat data, `safari_ios`
mirroring it) — and WebKit ignores the manifest's `orientation` field outright.
Fullscreen is the only piece that differs by device: iPhone has no
`Element.requestFullscreen`, iPad has had it since Safari 16.4. So even the
version that did lock was Android-only, which is half of why it went.

**A visitor whose device has rotation locked at the OS level turns the phone
and nothing happens, and the page cannot see that.** There is no API for the
setting. It is a Control Center toggle rather than a Settings page, so there is
nothing to deep-link to either — `prefs:root=` is a private scheme Apple
rejects apps for and Safari will not open it from a page at all. The one
detection that exists is comparing `screen.orientation.type` against the
accelerometer, and that needs `DeviceOrientationEvent.requestPermission()` on
iOS 14.5+, which is a system permission prompt. Showing someone a scary prompt
to diagnose a problem you then only tell them about is a bad trade.

So the card escalates on a timer instead. Eight seconds after it appears,
`#rotate .stuck` fades in under a rule with the sentence that would have needed
the detection. **Nothing is being detected and nothing needs to be**: a card
still up at eight seconds belongs either to someone who has not turned the
phone or to someone who turned it and got nothing, and the same sentence serves
both. Someone who simply rotates never sees it, because the card is gone
seconds earlier — and `dismissRotate` clears the timer, so it cannot fire into
a card that has already closed.

Which switch to name is decided by `screen.orientation.lock` being absent —
Control Center if it is, quick settings if it is not. That is a capability
test standing in for a platform, which is normally a smell, but here it is
exact: the card only ever shows on a touch device, and WebKit is the only touch
engine missing the API. A UA string would say the same thing less reliably.

It is a hint, not a gate. Portrait is not a broken state — `fit()` opens the
vertical FOV below 16:10 specifically so it is not (see §Camera) — so the card
has one button, the room behind it is already running, and `room-rotate-seen`
means it asks once. Turning the phone answers it too: a `matchMedia` listener on
`(orientation:landscape)` clears it, because someone who has done the thing
should not still be asked. The controls hint (`#onboard`) is held behind it and
released by whichever of those happens, since two cards asking two different
things in the same corner is not a layout the hud has.

The card sits at `z-index:8`, under `#load` (9) and `#nogl` (20). That is not
arbitrary: it means the card waits out the boot overlay without a timer, and a
device that could not start WebGL shows the WebGL message rather than an
invitation to rotate something that is not going to render either way.

## 8. If you change one thing, know this

- **Run the three checks. They take a second and need nothing installed.**

      python3 tools/links.py --check        # the link blocks match the data
      python3 tools/wallsheet.py --check    # the wall map matches the layout
      python3 tools/jscheck.py              # every shipped script parses

  All three run in CI on every push (`.github/workflows/check.yml`). `--check`
  regenerates `demos/wall-layout.js` in memory and compares it with the
  committed one, which is the "edited the layout and forgot to re-bake" case —
  previously detectable only by looking at the wall and noticing nothing had
  happened. It deliberately does not diff the JPEGs: they are drawn with
  whatever fonts the machine has, so their bytes are not reproducible across
  machines and a pixel diff would go red for reasons that have nothing to do
  with the change.
- **Project data lives in exactly one place.** Edit `portfolio-data.js`; both
  pages follow. Its links are the exception and live in `build_links.txt`, which
  `tools/links.py` writes back into it. If you add a project, add both
  thumbnails to `images/thumb/`, run `tools/links.py` for its link block, and if
  it belongs on a wall or a prop put it in `build_layout.txt` and re-run
  `tools/wallsheet.py`. A build matched by a prop's `auto` rule needs neither.
- **Every link a build has is a button.** Both the card rows and the room's
  readout used to stop after three, a cap from when the links were hand-written
  and nothing was going to add a fourth. Something was already behind it: the
  two netcode builds carry a second Youtube that had been in the data,
  unreachable from either page, the whole time. `build_links.txt` is edited to
  be shown, so everything in it is shown — `.lk` already wrapped, and the
  readout's
  row now wraps two-to-a-line at four or more.
- **A build can have no links at all.** `playOf` returns undefined there rather
  than every caller testing `g.links.length` first, `linkRow` renders nothing,
  and `hrefAttr` gives a whole-card anchor no `href` — an `<a>` with no href
  keeps its styling and is not focusable or clickable, which is the truth about
  a build with nothing to open.
- **A project has a `cat` and nothing else that classifies it.** The heading
  (`RESKIN`, `AR Games`), the short and long labels and the accent colour are
  the *category's*, and they are in `CAT` in `portfolio-shared.js`. Adding a
  category means adding it there, once: the filter buttons, the swatches, an
  `auto section=` rule in the layout and the baked section stripe all follow.
  `wallsheet.py` reads that block out of the file rather than restating the
  colours in Python, and `--check` fails on a `cat` that CAT does not have.
- **Anything both pages render belongs in `portfolio-shared.js`.** They load it
  as a classic script, so a helper defined in both is a redeclaration error and
  not merely a duplicate — `tools/jscheck.py` checks for that specifically.
  `esc`, `thumb`, `img`, `externalNote` and `linkRow` are already there. What
  is *not* shared is the card wrappers: the room's case file and the flat
  page's revealed card are genuinely different shapes, and folding them
  together would be inventing a template language to save four lines.
- **The shared scripts are loaded at a `?v=` and never by bare name.**
  `wallsheet.py` hashes `portfolio-data.js`, `portfolio-shared.js`,
  `wall-layout.js` and `asset-v.js` and rewrites the `<script src=>` on both
  pages; `--check` fails if a page is at the wrong one. This is the price of the rule above it.
  While every helper had a copy inline in the page that used it, a page and its
  helpers could not be from different releases. Now they can: a browser holding
  yesterday's `portfolio-shared.js` next to today's page does not degrade, it
  throws `ReferenceError` on the first helper the old copy has never heard of
  and the room boots into its error card. Hashed from content, so a rebuild
  that changes nothing changes no URL — the point is to make a stale copy
  unreachable, not to defeat caching. Do not hand-edit the `?v=`; re-run the
  tool. **Locally, serve with `tools/serve.py`**, which sends `Cache-Control:
  no-store` on everything. `python3 -m http.server` sends no cache headers at
  all, which lets a browser decide for itself how long a file stays fresh, and
  it is generous.
- **Every picture and every clip carries a content hash too, out of
  `demos/asset-v.js`.** This list used to stop at the scripts, on a note in
  `wallsheet.py` claiming everything else was versioned already — the sheets by
  `WALL_SHEET_V`, the models by `MODEL_V`, the modules by being named relative
  to the page. That note was wrong about the two biggest families on the site.
  A thumbnail is named by `thumb()` rewriting an image path and a preview by
  `CLIP_OF` handing back a slug, so neither URL moves when the file behind it is
  re-cropped or re-cut, and a browser holding the old bytes has no way to find
  out. Where it shows is the wall: the cover is baked into a JPEG that carries
  `WALL_SHEET_V` and updates, while the readout that opens over it and the panel
  behind that go on playing the previous cut and drawing the previous crop —
  which reads as the room zooming to a stale picture, and is how it was
  reported. `asset_versions()` hashes each file on its own rather than issuing
  one token over all of them: the two families are about 5 MB, and re-cutting
  one 60 kB clip should not evict ninety-six others from every cache that
  already holds them. `vsrc()` in `portfolio-shared.js` appends the hash, and
  both pages load the map — the flat page plays no clips but draws the same
  stills, and a map only one of them has is a stale picture on the other.
  `--check` fails if any hashed file has moved without the map being
  regenerated. None of this is visible under `tools/serve.py`, which sends
  `no-store`; it is only ever visible on the deployed site, which is the worst
  place to find out.
- **The room does not know how to slug a title, and must not learn.** The clip
  a build previews with is `CLIP_OF[title]` out of `wall-layout.js`, produced
  by the same run that named the files. There used to be three implementations
  of that one string transform — the room, `wallsheet.py`, `clips.py` — held
  together by a docstring asking them to agree. There is one now, in
  `tools/portfolio.py`, and it is on the build side only.
- **A zone's plane is shaped from its sheet, not alongside it.** `wallsheet.py`
  generates `aspect` per zone and the room does `planeH(Z, width, fallback)`.
  A width is a room-layout decision and stays in the room; a height is
  arithmetic. Do not put a literal height back — that is what made "change a
  grid, reshape its plane" two steps with only `#qa=1` between them.
- **The readout's state is one object and three cancels.** `hudState` holds all
  of it and `hudCancelSwap` / `hudCancelClose` / `hudCancelShutdown` are the
  only ways to drop a timer. Every bug this thing has had was the same bug — a
  path out of one state clearing the wrong subset of a dozen loose `let`s — and
  `if(x){clearTimeout(x);x=0}` appeared eight times before this. Add a timer,
  add its cancel; do not clear one inline.
- **The panel has two placement modes now.** Anything touching `placePanel()`
  or `focusOn()` has to work in both. Check a phone viewport, not just a narrow
  desktop window — `MOBILE_GPU` keys off `(pointer: coarse)` as well as size.
- **Panel-internal layout uses container queries, not media queries.** Reach
  for `@container panel` first; a viewport media query there will be wrong in
  one of the two modes. The query now sees real widths — 300px on the phone
  prop, ~1500px on the wall at 1080p — so a rule written for "the panel" has to
  be checked at both ends, not just the narrow one.
- **The panel's logical width is derived from where the camera is going.**
  `restPose()` is the single definition of that pose and `focusOn` and
  `panelWidth` both read it. If you change how `focusOn` frames a surface,
  change `restPose`, not the copy of the maths that used to live inline.
- **A 44px rule belongs in `@media (pointer:coarse)`, not in a width query.**
  A tablet is a touch device that is wider than any phone breakpoint.
- **Before adding a surface that lists builds, count the overlap.** Seventeen
  of the 33 already hang as cover art on zones 1, 8 and 9. A panel whose items
  are all up there is a second copy to keep in step, not a second route in —
  that is what retired MY GAMES and the netcode rack. Repeating a title is
  fine when the surface makes a different claim about it (`phone` is "shipped
  to a store", `holo` is "there is video"); it is not fine when the surface is
  just the same list again.
- **Two props overlapping is a measurement, not an opinion.** Project both
  through the live camera and intersect the rectangles; see the desk pass for
  the ten-line probe and the temporary `window.__m` hook that drives it. A
  prop that is fine on its own can be 100% behind the chair.
- **A prop chosen off its name renders as a box.** The atlas material is
  opaque for every mesh, so anything whose read depends on glass, a screen or
  an interior comes out flat. Convert it, place it, render it and look before
  writing anything about it.
- **A new prop is a `SCREENS` entry, not a new widget.** An invisible quad plus
  `addScreen` gets the halo, the raycast proxy, the Contents row, the deep
  link, the prev/next ring, Escape and the focus trap. Anything hand-rolled has
  to earn all of that back, and the window `wheel` handler will eat its scroll.
- **Give a new surface a `hud` and it changes what a tap does.** On touch a
  `hud` payload makes `activateHit` open the sheet instead of the panel. Set
  `hudHoverOnly` unless the card is one build with its own Play button.
- **The readout closes on a timer, not on a pointer leaving.** Anything touching
  `hudSet`, `hudHide` or the `pointermove` hook has to keep Play and Watch
  reachable: walk a pointer from a cover to the button and check it survives the
  trip, because the raycast under the gap will happily close the panel mid-walk.
- **The walls and the props are one file: `build_layout.txt`, then re-bake.**
  Nothing in `room-3d.html` or `tools/clips.py` carries a title list any more —
  `WALL_HERO_TITLES`, `CASUAL_TITLES` and the four `games.filter()` lines are
  all gone into it — and the `?v=` is a content hash rather than a number to
  remember. `listFor()` in the room keeps each prop's old filter as a fallback,
  so the room still says what it is if the generated file never loads. Editing the layout
  without running `python3 tools/wallsheet.py` changes nothing on the wall —
  that is the intended failure mode, not a bug to route around. `tools/serve.py`
  runs the bake for you while you work; it does not remove the step.
- **A zone's grid belongs to the zone**, and lives in `ZONES` in
  `tools/wallsheet.py`, not in the layout. A build moved from zone 1 to zone 8
  renders at zone 8's cell size, and each zone's list has to fit `cols*rows`
  cells, less one where the zone carries a plate. Changing a grid changes the
  shape of that zone's sheet, so the plane in `room-3d.html` has to be reshaped
  with it — `#qa=1` fails on the aspect if you forget. Below about 90 screen
  pixels a cover stops reading as a screenshot, which is what ruled out the
  original 8x5.
- **`og.jpg` is a photograph of the back wall.** `tools/ogimage.py` crops a real
  headless capture of the room, so reordering zone 1 makes the link preview
  wrong. Nothing can detect this; `tools/wallsheet.py` prints a reminder and
  that is all it can do. `tools/og-source.png` is gitignored, so there is no
  original to re-crop — it has to be re-shot.
- **A new cover needs a clip, and a clip needs looking at.** Bake it, pull the
  first frame, and check it is neither a pillarboxed portrait capture nor a
  black rectangle before hanging it.
- **Weather multiplies, it never assigns.** Every field in `WEATHER` is a
  factor or an addend on something `applyTime` has already worked out from
  `STOPS`. Anything that reaches into the weather and sets a colour or an
  intensity outright has put the two systems in a position to disagree, and the
  hour will win on the next frame anyway. Scale `sun.intensity` and most of the
  room follows for free.
- **Do not move the weather onto the click.** It has been considered and the
  argument is written down under Weather: no visible cause, noise behind an open
  panel, and — decisively — `focusOn` points the camera away from the glass, so
  it would fire where it cannot be seen.
- **Rain falls with `map.offset.y += `, not `-=`.** Three.js samples
  `uv * repeat + offset`, so a rising offset moves a streak DOWN the quad. Do
  not "correct" this from first principles — measure it, and calibrate the
  measurement's sign against a known synthetic shift first.
- **Lightning's three gates are a contract, not tuning.** Reduced motion, no
  panel or readout open, and `fDur+400` minimum between strikes. The last is
  WCAG 2.3.1 and is a separate check from the random interval on purpose; it has
  to stay longer than the longest strike, which is why it is derived from `fDur`
  and not a constant.
- **The strokes inside a strike must never take the glow back to dark.** That is
  what makes a four-stroke strike one flash rather than four, and it is the only
  reason the restrikes are allowed to exist at all. Modulate the envelope, never
  restart it.
- **The flash curve lives in `stepFlash` only.** `applyTime` reads `flashK`
  straight. Putting a square or an ease back at that end compounds with the
  envelope and quietly turns a 900ms fade into a snap again.
- **The weather walk keeps its direction.** Turning `wDir` back into a coin flip
  restores the nine-transition hitting time and the storm silently stops
  happening — which is a bug nobody can see, because the failure mode is an
  effect that simply never fires. If the pacing needs changing, simulate it; the
  numbers in the Weather section were produced that way, not measured by
  watching.
- **`1` must never lose the `fDur+400` floor.** Key auto-repeat is what turns
  that line from a debug affordance into a strobe. Verified in-browser: 40
  keydowns dispatched 150ms into a live strike, and 40 more at 550ms, fired
  exactly zero new strikes.
- **The inter-strike floor is derived from `fDur` and has to stay that way.**
  Every time the lifetime grew, a constant floor became wrong — 900, then 1400,
  then unfixable once the lifetime became a 2.3–6.8s roll.
- **Anything additive on the glass can only add light.** That is why the bead
  sprite is a highlight and not a ball: a filled disc of added light is a
  glowing sphere, and two versions of the sprite were lost to relearning it. If
  a water effect needs to be *darker* than what is behind it, additive is the
  wrong blend and no amount of tuning the gradient will get there.
- **`wet` is not `W.rain` and the difference is the point.** It lags on purpose,
  four seconds up and twenty-one down. Anything that should outlast the cloud —
  beads, deck sheen, future puddles — reads `wet`; anything that is the rain
  itself reads `W.rain`. Collapsing them would make the room dry the instant a
  front passes, which is the one thing weather never does.
- **The city layers write no depth.** They are transparent materials, so nothing
  drawn after them is occluded by them. Any new ground-level plate outside the
  glass will paint straight over the skyline — that is what killed the wet-deck
  sheen, and it will kill the next one the same way. Prefer changing the
  materials that are already at the right depth.
- **A layer's contribution is measurable; measure it before believing in it.**
  Scale the mesh to zero, capture, scale it back, capture, and subtract — with a
  same-state control pair first, because the monitors, the holo and the city
  windows churn about 7% of the frame between any two captures on their own. The
  sheen looked like it was working right up until it was differenced.
- **`--screenshot` with `--virtual-time-budget` does not finish on this scene.**
  Under swiftshader it has to render every virtual frame in the budget, and a
  1500ms budget did not produce a PNG in four minutes. Drive CDP instead:
  `Page.captureScreenshot` over the debugger socket returns in under a second
  and `Runtime.evaluate` reads any value out of the module directly. Launch with
  `--remote-debugging-port`, find the tab through `/json/list`, and speak
  RFC6455 at its `webSocketDebuggerUrl` — that is about ninety lines of Python
  with no dependencies, and it replaces the `document.title` polling trick
  entirely.
- **A headless window smaller than 600px on its short side is `MOBILE_GPU`.**
  A 900x600 window has an innerHeight around 516, which silently takes the
  mobile branch — the first round of far-sheet measurements was taken on a
  build that had never created a far sheet.
- **Audio is a function of `W`, like everything else on the glass.** The bed's
  gains are computed from `W.rain` and `windLean` in the same frame the sheets
  are. Giving the sound its own intensity state would let the window and the
  speakers disagree about the weather, which is worse than having no sound.
- **"On by default" means armed, not playing.** No browser will start audio
  before a real gesture, and nothing is built until one arrives — a visitor who
  muted last time gets no `AudioContext` at all. Do not "fix" this by creating a
  suspended context at load.
- **A filter without a ceiling is not a band.** One biquad is 12dB/octave, which
  two octaves up is still audible: the rain hiss measured a 9.1kHz centroid on a
  lone highpass and needed two cascaded lowpass stages to reach 4.4kHz. Raising Q
  puts a resonant peak where the problem is, it does not remove it.
- **Narrowing a band costs level.** A distant strike is attenuated by its gain
  term and again by its lowpass, and the second one is invisible in the code.
  Measure the output, not the gain value.
- **The audio graph is measurable headlessly.** Tap an `AnalyserNode` onto
  `sMaster` or `sThun` and read RMS and spectral centroid — the graph renders
  under a null audio device, and `Input.dispatchMouseEvent` over CDP counts as a
  real gesture for autoplay. Every number in the Sound section came out that way.
  It cannot tell you whether something sounds *good*; it can tell you whether it
  is rain or static, and that is most of the failures.
- **Nothing in the clock bar may change width on hover.** It is centred with
  `translateX(-50%)`, so a control that grows slides the entire bar sideways
  under the cursor reaching for it. Anchor popups absolutely instead, and keep
  the gap between trigger and popup inside the hoverable box or the pointer
  crosses dead space and closes it en route.
- **A `visibility:hidden` subtree cannot be focused.** A hover popup that opens
  on `:focus-within` is only keyboard-reachable by tabbing from a control that
  is already visible — which is fine, but it means the trigger has to be in the
  tab order immediately before it, and testing it by calling `.focus()` on the
  hidden element directly will silently do nothing and look like a bug.
- **Run `#qa=1` after any panel change** and compare against the baseline you
  measure, not a number written down here — see the QA section for why.
